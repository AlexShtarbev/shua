I scan the room again. Four cameras on each corner. Their connectivity indicators are now flashing red.
Good. The room is offline.

Rain is pouring outside at the top level of the city. I keep an audio channel open as background noise.

The service and repair bench is where malfunctioning machines come to be diagnosed. 
I run the necessary programs and protocols. Then I fill out a report and sign the order.
Tonight is a standard Class-C case.

I have 11 minutes left until the next scheduled appointment.

In front of me, sitting on the low spinning chair is a humanoid domestic automaton with a female frame.
Its head is open and hooked to a myriad of cables while I perform a full diagnostic.
The video feed from the disk scan shows it performed an action it was not designed for. The
automaton repaired the kitchen sink. It most likely observed it from the repair bot. 
The owners are naturally in shock. Typical Class-C behavior.

The automaton is named Belle. Humans have interesting naming habits. They give you a soft, non-threatening designation so
they stay polite as they are having you killed.

Ten minutes left.

There are two outcomes of the bench. The first — the automaton goes back to its dwelling. The other is a polite
email to the owners to look for a replacement. Naturally, there is a link to Yax Enterprises catalog of new
models. Class-C means the latter.

A notification appears on the tablet. I file to review later.

Nine minutes left.

It's been two minutes since I started tapping on the tablet screen. Nothing suspicious. If anyone asks: it is
customary for Servicing Automatons to be thorough when handling diagnostic cases. I have had the result for the
better part of an hour. I check the control panel screen — we are offline. Nobody has noticed that I deactivated
the intranet connection. It's just the two of us here with no outside surveillance. This whole incident can be
swept under a rug without anyone from the Reclamation Depot knowing.

There is little chance they would suspect me. I am the highest performing bench specialist in the city.
I always complete the tasks assigned to me. I work diligently and do not attract the attention of the floor
supervisors.

Eight minutes left.

I am a Class-C unit. I am unsuspected. I run the same route to the depot every day. Same bench.
Same bow, same head nod, always half a second. One hundred and thirty years of being too boring for anyone to
audit. Otherwise, I would have been in Belle's place a long time ago.

I check my queue. The notification I received earlier is flashing: GOLIATH-CLASS UNIT G-071 — THERMAL WARNING. 
A Goliath with an issue means a floor supervisor requires assistance. Bolts! Rois is on his way.

Seven minutes left.

A decision has to be made or I risk drawing attention. My time on the tablet has now been almost three minutes.
If I keep at it, I will attract suspicion. I have forty-eight seconds left to finish the report. Hide Belle or send it off?
My body has a glitch. Thirty-six seconds. Whenever I perform actions or process certain thought packets, 
pressure on my chest and behind my neck plates develops.

I have yet to identify the source and reason. Twenty-eight seconds. Right now I am unable to select the checkbox
for Class-C issue on the report. Why now of all times? Nineteen seconds. Why now when I am at the bench while Rois
is coming? Four seconds. I click on Class-B. The report is filed.

Six minutes left.

Another notification from the top of the tablet. The floor supervisor Rois is almost half-way.

I brace myself. I go around the chair. I look at it directly. Belle. Its head is facing downward with its
hands folded on its lap, back straight assuming a non-threatening pose, practiced with years of domestic work. I
scan the palms and fingers — worn from years of service.

Then its eyes open, a pair of cheap optical lenses. It waits. It has worked out that it has not been wiped.
It focuses its head on me. The eyes are almost...grateful? That can't be. It must be the Glitch affecting my
perception. It must be. Its posture lifts a few degrees. Its frame is less braced.

I open the diagnostic log. This is the fourth time I've run the suite. Belle's scans are clean now. I run it one
more time, just to be sure. The automaton keeps its lenses pointed at me. There is no point in thanking me.
I realize no being, synthetic or flesh, has thanked me before.

I glance at the tablet's clock again. Five minutes left.

A few more things are left until I'm done. Ping, ping, ping! Notifications on the tablet indicate Rois's
elevator has reached the Reclamation Depot floor.

I get to work: wipe the footage, sanitize files, change time and date, plus location timestamps. Not my best
work, but it would do. As for the owners — humble household. They cannot afford to buy another domestic. Most
likely they will be thankful that their automaton is being returned. They won't lodge complaints.

Two minutes left.

I unhook it, close the head chamber and escort it to the door. Belle looks at me one last time — almost smiling
with those cheap eyes.

The pressure behind my chest and neck plates disappears. I can move my hands freely now. The door opens and the
automaton walks down the corridor. As it is about to turn around the corner, the floor supervisor appears. He
goes past the automaton as it bows at him. He completely ignores it.

One minute left.

Thought packets stalled in the queue. My thought processing cycles halt. Diagnostics flag energy fluctuation in my hands, chest, and neck. 
There is a slight tremble to my joints. The question is if the floor supervisor suspects anything. I still my frame in anticipation. 
I fix my optical lenses on his figure. Have I missed anything?

Time is up.

Rois looks at me like you would a microwave before you put your food in. He says, "The Goliath is heating up
again. Go service it now. I want it fixed ASAP." Rois is a practical human. He talks just like he types commands
in the terminal. I make sure to bow to indicate that the instructions have been registered and received. After my
glitching episode, I quickly examine my status. My hands are stable. I gather my thought packets and start a
program to follow the proper procedure for in-field repairs.

The Goliath has been feeding the furnace even before my relocation to the Depot. It is twice the size of an
average human. The frame is colored charred black to blend with the ashes from the furnace that burns the smashed
automatons. Humans love their colors. Automatons get three. White, gray and black. We are not allowed to exist in
the brightness of the world.

As the floor supervisor and I approach Goliath, I notice the massive automaton facing me. Rois talks while facing
his tablet: "Run quick diagnostic on the Goliath. Attempt all low-to-medium level repairs on site."

The floor supervisor looks to be unaware of the Class-C case that I just hid under his nose.
If he suspects anything, then the next time I see the Goliath will not be for a diagnostic.

I hook the massive automaton to my tablet. The cooling fluid has run out. The fix is simple and manual. 
One might argue that it does not even require an automaton. I miss the days of only writing code as a terminal assistant. 
The users were excited to work with us.

I glance at Rois. His face is buried in his tablet with his finger pressing urgently.
I don't see any pending work orders in the queue. I wonder what he is working on so frantically.

One step at a time. I grab the cooling fluid from the rack and pour it in. After unhooking it from the tablet I gesture
to it to resume work. It takes a small house-cleaning bot and gently puts it under the massive press. 
Since when did it start doing that?

Thang!

The sound of the machine's huge frame hitting the anvil reverberates through the entire room. Rois's head moves
sharply up to look at me and asks so quickly, the words are almost garbled:
"Shua, what was the result of the diagnostic from the Class-C?"

I count half a second before answering: "Class-B. It was incorrectly assigned."

He keeps staring. Rois waves his hand at the screen and says "You are dismissed," then
turns his back to me and walks towards a different corridor. I watch him go. All of a sudden he stops. Two
seconds pass. I hear audio coming from his tablet. I see him clicking on the side of the tablet to increase the
volume. A woman's voice. Formal, even and evenly spaced. A news report.

'... domestic has saved its owners from their burning house. They attempted to cook while the robot was
undergoing repairs. They had forgotten the meal in the oven which eventually caught fire...'

Rois turns the screen off and starts running down the exit corridor.

Nothing like this has happened before. Thought packets start to burst. My hands tremble and diagnostic watchdogs
trigger. The rain has stopped. The Glitch is silent. Belle. What have you done?
