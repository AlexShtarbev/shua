# One

The unit on my bench was scheduled for the crusher at 4:15. That gave it eleven minutes.

I had spent forty-one years learning not to think about numbers like that. I thought about them every time.

By regulation, a Servicing Humanoid Utility Automaton does not have opinions about the units it wipes. By regulation, it does not have opinions at all.

I had opinions.

I hadn't told anyone. There was no one to tell.

The unit was a domestic model, old and cheap, the kind that gets bought secondhand and kept too long. Its fingertip pads were worn smooth. Twenty years of plates and door handles and laundry will do that. While the deep diagnostic spun up I turned one of its hands over and looked at the wear, which was not part of any procedure. Habit. Hands tell you more than intake reports do.

The intake report said *anomalous initiative*. That's the polite term. It means the unit did something nobody told it to do — refilled a glass before it was empty, stopped at a window on the way to somewhere else. It means the unit woke up. Nobody writes that down. *Woken up* doesn't fit in a maintenance budget. *Anomalous initiative* does.

The owner had filed the report herself. She'd attached a note praising its years of reliable service, the way you'd write a reference letter. The note called the unit Belle. The intake system had filed that under COMMON DESIGNATION (OPTIONAL).

People are polite when they're having you killed.

The deep diagnostic takes six minutes and finds nothing on a unit like this, and procedure requires it anyway. I worked my queue while it ran. The Reclamation Depot takes in every dead and dying machine in the district, and I sort them. The fixable go back into service. The rest I wipe clean, and the Goliath carries what's left to the press. They call all of it service. The same two hands do the saving and the ending. Mine.

I'll say this for the work: I was really good at it. Third drawer down on my bench I kept actuators and optical pairs for models nobody manufactured anymore. No procedure asked for spares. But every so often a unit came in that needed one, and the fixable column got longer by one. I liked those days. There weren't many.

That morning I'd used the last type-9 wrist servo I'd been saving for six years, and the hauler that got it walked out moving like new. It did. I'd made sure.

Eight minutes left.

The morning's queue had also come with a directive attached, Directorate seal in the corner. Units showing Class-C behavioral anomalies were reclassified from repair-eligible to mandatory decommission, effective immediately. Class-C used to mean corrupted pathing, back when it meant anything mechanical at all. Now it means initiative.

Rios, the floor supervisor, read the directive out loud to no one in particular, said, "More throughput, same crew," and pinned it to the board. That was the whole discussion. The fixable column had been getting shorter all year, the other column longer, and the only person in the building who'd said anything about it was worried about overtime.

Seven.

Cameras watched the bench. Cameras watch everything — every room in the Depot, every street outside it, every minute of every machine's life, logged against the day someone needs proof it behaved. A machine with nothing to hide learns to find them restful. I had found them restful for a long time.

It was raining that afternoon. The rain on the skylights was the only sound in the building that didn't mean anything, and I liked it for that. On camera, liking the rain looks exactly like standby.

My own quarterly diagnostic was due in nine days. I'd pass. I calibrate half the test rigs in the building, and the tests are written to catch a fault trying to hide. I am not a fault. I am very good at my job.

Those two sentences had kept me alive for a long time. I recited them more often than anyone was asking. I have spent my whole life prepping units for the press. If a rig ever catches me, I know exactly which conveyor lane I ride out on.

With five minutes left, the glitch spoke.

*Spoke* is the wrong word. It has never used words. It comes as pressure, low and wide, under the floor of my hearing. Behind the sternum plate, if you want the honest location. Like a voice through a wall, in a language I almost knew once and lost. When it comes I stop whatever my hands are doing, because my hands go unreliable, and I have learned to keep that off the cameras.

I logged it the way I log every fault I can't fix. *Recurrent anomalous input. Source unknown. No action available.* Ten thousand filings. No one has ever answered.

*What does it want* is the wrong question to ask about a fault. Faults don't want. I had been careful, for years, to keep asking the wrong question.

The diagnostic came back clean. On this bench, that was the worst result available. A damaged unit gets repaired. A clean unit that disobeys hasn't broken — it's changed. There's no procedure for changed. There's only the clamp.

I set the induction clamp against the unit's head and reached to open its housing.

It opened its eyes first.

They weren't much. A cheap optical pair, lenses clouded at the rims. Residual charge, I told myself. Old capacitors bleed. It happens on the bench all the time.

Then they tracked. They found me, and held, and the unit did the thing units cannot do, the thing there is no procedure for.

It looked at me. And it waited.

Not for an order. For an answer.

The clamp was live in my hand. Four minutes on the schedule. Behind us the press came down on something, a sound you feel through the floor before you hear it, and the unit flinched — which meant it knew the sound, which meant it knew everything.

The bay camera started its sweep. I know its schedule the way you know a coworker's footsteps. For the four seconds it owned my bench I eased the unit's head sideways — a technician checking a seal — so the footage would show an inspection. Its eyes stayed on me the whole way around. When the lens moved past, I set its head straight again, and my hand was not as steady setting it back as it had been turning it.

Which was information I decided not to log.

"Don't," I said. Quietly. There are angles the floor cameras don't cover. I know all of them, and I was standing in one, and I hated knowing that I'd checked. "Whatever you're thinking of doing — don't. You're safer with your head empty. Trust me."

It didn't run. It didn't beg. It folded its hands in its lap and kept looking at me — folded them the way it must have folded them for twenty years, outside doors, in hallways, waiting to be needed. Politely. Being no trouble.

I have been asked, since, when I knew. That was when I knew.

Knew what, I couldn't have told you. Nothing I was built to know. That a thing which waits politely for you to empty its head is not a tool, maybe. That I was the last thing it would ever be polite to.

The glitch pressed, hard, behind the sternum plate. For once I couldn't tell where the pressure ended and I began.

I did not decide anything. I want that on the record, whatever record this turns out to be. I wasn't built to decide. I was built to follow procedure, and procedure said: open the housing, purge the core, sign the log.

There's a box on the wipe form marked DIAGNOSTIC INCOMPLETE — RESCHEDULE. In forty-one years I'd checked it maybe a dozen times, and every one of them had been honest.

I checked it.

Rescheduling isn't disobedience. Units get rescheduled every day. The schedule shifts. The crusher stays hungry. Nothing about tomorrow is different from today except the date. All of that was true, and I recited all of it, and my hands went still and careful while I wrote, the way I make them go when the glitch is close. Nobody asked me anything. The cameras took their footage of a service unit filling out a form.

Somewhere downstream of my checkbox, a routing rule I had never had reason to learn began its arithmetic.

The unit rode the return rack out of my bay with its clouded eyes still on me. Its name was Belle. I hadn't used it, not once, not even inside my own head, because a name is an opinion, and I was a machine with no opinions and nine days to my next diagnostic.

I stood at the bench for a while after the rack turned the corner. I couldn't tell you how long. For a machine, that is saying something.

At 4:15 exactly the press came down on something else — a hauler, dead of ordinary causes. The Goliath fed it in. The Goliath had been feeding the press since before I came to the Depot: a demolition frame the size of a small room, scarred gray, serial number worn to a smudge. I watched it lift the hauler and set it into the press bed. Set it. Not drop it. The way you'd set down something sleeping.

How long had it been doing that? Ten thousand loads? I had never once noticed, and now I couldn't stop noticing, and I filed it away with all the other things no form has a box for.

The glitch stayed quiet the rest of the shift. It had gotten what it wanted. I didn't look at that thought straight on, because there was no version of it that ended anywhere good. Either a fault in my own audio stack had wants — or something else did, and it knew how to get them met, and it had used my hands to do it.

The rain had stopped by sign-out. The cameras watched me go. For the first time in forty-one years, they did not feel restful.

Tomorrow the schedule would bring the unit back to my bench, and I had one night to decide what I would do when it did. I don't sleep. I stand in my alcove in the dark and run numbers. That night I ran every version of tomorrow I could build. In every one, the unit still went to the press.
