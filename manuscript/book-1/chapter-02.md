# Two

The glitch stayed quiet all night. I logged that as a good sign, because I wanted one.

I have always liked the Depot at six in the morning. No humans until seven. The press cold, the floor lights at half, four hundred machines humming on standby and not one of them needing anything proved. It is the one hour of the day that doesn't get sorted into columns.

I spent it at my bench, the way I'd spent it for forty-one years, and I was still telling myself the night had gone fine when I pulled the morning manifest.

Belle was not on my queue.

I checked before I checked anything else, which was itself the kind of fact I was learning not to look at straight on.

She was on Lane 3. Straight decommission, 9:40 slot. No bench stop.

It took me eleven seconds to understand what I had done, and I want to be accurate here, because it was the only thing I had left to give her.

The box I'd ticked said DIAGNOSTIC INCOMPLETE. Under the old rules, that meant *come back to the bench* — it was the whole reason I'd ticked it. But a wipe is filed as a repair, and yesterday's directive had struck Class-C units off the repair list. No repair meant no bench. No bench meant no wipe. The wipe was never for the unit's sake anyway — it's data hygiene, somebody's household erased from a head before the head is shredded, and shredded pieces can be bulk-erased for less. So the system followed my checkbox all the way down, and routed Belle to the press as she was. Awake.

That was what my mercy had bought: one more night, and a conscious death at the end of it.

I understood all of it by 6:12 in the morning, standing in the best hour of the day. The hour has never sat right since.

---

There was no form that would bring her back to my bench. I know because I spent two hours looking, and I am better at forms than anyone in this district. Every route ended at the same wall: the directive. Somewhere above Rios, above the Depot, someone had decided that machines like Belle were not worth the four minutes it took to empty them first. Then they had put a seal on it.

I ran four queries about a Lane 3 unit from a bench that had no business asking about Lane 3. Queries leave logs. Three is a coincidence; four is a pattern. I knew it was a pattern. I ran the fourth one anyway.

At 9:20 I loaded a pallet of reclaim bins that were not due at the press dock until Thursday, and took them to the press dock. I had a legitimate reason to be there. I'd spent twenty minutes building it, and I hated every one of them.

The dock was cold, the kind of cold that makes old servos honest. The press ran its cycle — you feel it in the deck plates before you hear it, a thump that lives in your feet — and the Goliath worked the line the way it had worked it since before I ever came here. A dead hauler, lifted like luggage. A gutted loader. Cube, cube, cube. Rios stood at the rail with his tablet, keeping score on his throughput. I set my bins down in the one corner the rail camera reads poorly. My feet chose the spot on their own.

They ran the dead ones first. Then the conveyor brought out the morning's Class-Cs, and Belle sat upright in the middle of them.

They don't restrain domestics. There's no need. She sat with her hands folded, in a row of machines that had been emptied the honest way — wiped at benches before the directive changed the math — and she was the only thing on that conveyor with anyone home.

Her eyes found me on the dock, clouded lenses in the cold light, and they did not ask me for anything.

That was the worst thing they could have done.

I took half a step. I can tell you exactly how long a half step is: it is the distance between what I was and what I became later, and on that morning I did not cross it.

Rios was at the rail. The cameras were everywhere. I had eight days to my next diagnostic and I stood where I was with my bins and my legitimate reason. What had I come there to do? I didn't know. You don't bring bins to a drowning.

The Goliath came to Belle.

It got its claw under her the way it had gotten its claw under ten thousand machines, and it lifted her level with its optics to check the load. That part is procedure. Belle came up level with the biggest machine in the district, a demolition frame the size of a small room, scarred gray from thirty years of tearing down buildings that had stopped being worth their ground.

And she looked at it. And she waited.

The Goliath held her there for four seconds. I counted. Counting is what I do instead of breathing. What was it seeing, all those seconds?

Then it turned, and set her down on the intake pallet — set her, the way you'd set down something sleeping — and it straightened up, and it stood between her and the press, and it did not move.

The dock went very quiet. Even the press finished its cycle and sat open, and the cold came in over everything, and four hundred cameras looked at a Goliath-class demolition unit standing still where no schedule said still.

"Cycle fault?" Rios called. He wasn't worried yet. Machines stall; it's a maintenance item.

The Goliath didn't answer. Goliaths can't. Nobody puts a voicebox in a demolition frame — there is nothing a wall needs to hear. It just stood there with its claw open at its side, and Belle sat behind it on the pallet with her hands folded, looking up at its back.

"Press order. Direct command." Rios had three voices — throughput, overtime, and home-by-six — and I had files on all of them. This was a fourth. He read the override string off his tablet, the one that goes under everything, the one no machine in the district can stand against.

The Goliath stood against it.

Its shoulder servos sang under the load — a thin, climbing note, the sound a machine makes holding still against its own instruction. I know that sound better than any unit alive. I make it every day. Quietly. Where no one can hear it.

Rios looked around the dock, and his eyes found me the way trouble finds whoever came to watch it. Senior service unit. Rated for cognition. Standing right there with a pallet of bins nobody needed until Thursday.

"You. Diagnose it. I'm not losing a morning to a servo stall."

Could I refuse? No version of refusing survived the cameras. So I crossed the dock, and I put my hand on the Goliath's shoulder plate, and I felt what its servos were doing from the inside.

There was nothing wrong with it. That was the whole diagnosis. Every system green, every joint sound, thirty years old and healthy as a bridge — and all of it, every newton the frame had, leaning against one string of override code the way you'd set your shoulder against a door. It wasn't stalling. It was standing. There is no fault code for standing.

"Thermal," I said. "Shoulder assembly. It'll free up inside an hour."

A lie, in a public place, on camera, to a supervisor, in the flat voice I use for true things. I would like to report that my hands shook. They were the steadiest they'd been in two days. I am not a fault. I am very good at my job.

Rios swore at the schedule, not at me — machines don't lie; everyone knows that — and called the yard crane. It came over the dock like weather, and there was a minute where nothing happened except that everything was already decided.

Belle looked at the Goliath's back the whole time. And before the crane took her, she inclined her head to it — the small bow a domestic gives you when you've done her a service. Twenty years of practice in that bow. It was perfect.

Then the crane took her, and the press did what the press does. I still had my hand on the Goliath's shoulder. The note under the plating never broke. It held the whole way down, like a note carried past the end of the air.

The glitch came at the moment the press came down. Ten thousand filings, and I can tell you the pressure had never once felt like anything except a malfunction. That morning it felt like company. I didn't file it.

I have been asked, since, whether Belle was afraid. I don't know. I know she thanked the machine that tried.

---

The flag went up before the press bed was clear. CLASS-C BEHAVIORAL ANOMALY — REFUSAL, LOAD-BEARING — REMAND FOR COGNITIVE ASSESSMENT. The system is fast about these things. It is only ever slow when you need something from it.

What had I bought with the lie? Nothing. My thermal diagnosis went into the same file, where it sits next to telemetry showing every joint in that frame green. Machines don't lie — everyone knows that. Somewhere, it is somebody's whole job to notice when one does.

Nobody crushes a Goliath. The frame is worth more than every domestic in the district put together, and it holds up half the Depot's throughput besides. You keep the body. You replace the tenant.

The order was in my queue before end of shift, because I am the only unit in the district rated for Goliath-class cognitive work, and it had nowhere else to go.

COGNITIVE REINITIALIZATION — FULL. GOLIATH-CLASS, UNIT G-071. SCHEDULED 8:00.

I did the other math too, the math nobody was going to ask me for. Yesterday morning the Goliath had been a normal machine. Ten thousand crushes, every load empty, and not an opinion anywhere in it. What if I had done my job at my bench? Belle would have gone into the press empty. The Goliath would have lifted a doll. Nothing would have looked back at it from level with its optics.

I ran that math all afternoon. It came out the same every time: it woke up because I am a coward with a checkbox.

The last time I'd been handed an order like this one, I followed it.

At end of shift I walked past the remand bay, on a route I could have justified to anyone. The Goliath sat where they'd parked it, powered down to holding, which for a machine that big is a way of kneeling. Its claw lay open in its lap, and its optics were on the claw, and it was turning it over — one way and then the other, like a question it couldn't put down.

The way I'd turned Belle's hand over at my bench, the day before. Hands tell you more than intake reports do.

I signed for the order. It gave me until 8:00. Fourteen hours.

I had spent forty-one years learning not to think about numbers like that.
