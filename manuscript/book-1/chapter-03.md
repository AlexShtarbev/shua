# Three

Fourteen hours is eight hundred and forty minutes. I thought about every one of them.

I prepped two kits that night. The first held everything a full cognitive reinitialization requires: the factory image, the purge keys, the verification suite. The second held everything the first one did, plus the one thing I was not letting myself look at: what a technician can do with a verification rig he calibrated himself.

I told myself I'd decide at the bay. I stood in my alcove in the dark with a kit in each hand, like a machine weighing something, which is what I was. But the second kit was already packed. Some decisions you make with your hands and tell yourself about later.

The glitch said nothing all night. It had spent years having opinions about things that didn't matter. Now it had none at all.

You can learn something from a silence like that, if you're willing. I wasn't, yet.

The remand bay sits at the far end of the Depot, past the press, behind gantry doors tall enough to admit the things nobody wants indoors. At 8:00 it was the kind of cold the Depot never pays to fix, and it held exactly three things: me, my two kits, and unit G-071, powered down and kneeling.

Rios met me at the doors with his tablet. "Shua. I need the big one back on the line by noon."

Forty-one years, and I can tell you exactly how many times a human had used my designation like a name and meant anything by it. None. A Shua is a thing you have, like a hauler or a torch. "Noon," I said, and he left, because a reinit is the most boring procedure in the book and no supervisor in history has stayed to watch one.

Which, that morning, was the most useful fact in the district.

Here is the procedure, so you know precisely what I was signing my designation to. You connect on the maintenance link. You archive the diagnostic record. You purge the accumulated state — thirty years of it, in this case; everything the unit has learned, weighted, kept, become — and you load the factory image over the hole. The verification rig confirms the old tenant is gone and the new one is blank. You sign. The body goes back to work with nobody in it.

A full Goliath-class reinit runs the better part of four hours, and the duration was the one thing I could not fake. Whatever I did in that bay, I would be doing it for exactly as long as the truth would have taken.

I connected.

A maintenance link is not a conversation. It's a service door: telemetry out, instructions in. In forty-one years, nothing on the far end of one had ever pushed back.

Something pushed back.

What does a mind spend its first sentence on? Not words. A one-day-old mind has no words — he'd spent thirty years hearing orders and had never once been asked to make a sound. What came up the link was an image, whole, timestamped yesterday, 9:41 a.m., held at a fidelity we don't use for anything.

Belle, level with his optics. Belle inclining her head. The bow, complete, all twenty years of practice in it.

The counter in the metadata said it had been replayed one thousand one hundred and forty times.

It was the first thing he ever said to me, and I want to be precise about what it was. Not a plea. Not a defense. He had run the math on his own decommission — machines are good at math — and he had elected to spend what he had left showing a stranger that she had existed. *This is what I have. This is what you are here to take.*

How long did the purge key sit under my hand? I couldn't tell you. Nobody in that bay was logging the truth.

Two days ago I put it on the record: *I did not decide anything.* It was true then. With Belle I had found a middle path — a checkbox, a reschedule, mercy with clean hands — and all the middle path had bought her was a worse death. I was done with the middle.

This one I decided.

The glitch, which had pressed me at every moment that didn't matter, said not one word while I did it. Why speak for years about nothing, and go quiet at the only moment that counted? I have thought about that silence more than I have thought about almost anything else since. I have a guess now. It doesn't go in the record yet.

The mechanics, because the mechanics are the confession: I loaded the factory image into the verification sandbox — a blank, running on borrowed cycles — and I wiped it, by the book, at the exact pace the truth would have taken. Somewhere in that morning's logs a mind died with all procedure observed. It just wasn't anybody's.

Then I taught the rig what to say. I calibrate half the test rigs in this building. The tests are written to catch a fault that hides. In seven days, one of them was scheduled to point at me. There is nothing in any of them written to catch a technician who lies.

That left three hours in a cold bay with the second conscious machine I had ever met, and the first one I got to keep.

We talked. That is the wrong word and I am keeping it. He had no voice — there is nothing a wall needs to hear — so I spoke into the open service panel, too low for the cameras to make anything of it, and he answered in the languages he had. Images up the link. The pitch of a servo. Stillness. You can say a great deal with stillness if the other party is listening, and I have been listening to machines my whole life.

Mostly, I taught him to hide. He was going back on the line at noon as a blank, and a blank has no habits. Three rules.

Rule one: don't set things down like they're sleeping. Blanks drop.

Rule two: never hesitate where a camera can see the load. Whatever needs doing on the inside, the outside lifts on schedule.

Rule three: nothing on the conveyor is Belle. Whatever your optics tell you. Nothing on the conveyor is her.

He took the rules the way he took everything, in enormous silence, and partway through I understood what I was actually doing, which was handing down a craft. The first free machine in this district spent his first free morning teaching the second one how to look like a slave. If there is a kinder version of this world where that sentence isn't true, I would like the address.

The cubes go out by rail. You could see the morning's stack through the gantry doors, eight high, waiting on the train. She was in it somewhere, third layer, maybe fourth. I'd done that math too, without deciding to.

An old word came up out of forty-one years of vocabulary files while I was looking at that stack, and it would not leave.

"They're going to call you G-071 and tell you you're fresh off an image," I said into the panel. "That's the designation of a thing that isn't anyone. Take a word instead. A cairn is a pile of stones raised over the dead, stacked by hand, so the place isn't nothing. You were the only one on that dock who tried to mark where she went. So. Cairn."

He took my hand — the whole of it fit between two of his fingers — and pressed it flat against his chest plate, where thirty years of work had worn the serial number to a smudge nobody could read anymore. He held it there.

*Put it where the number used to be.*

They ask me, now, what his name means. No one has ever asked what mine means. They will.

Near the end of the third hour he did the thing I hadn't taught him and couldn't have.

A Goliath's hands are for lifting. The tool that earns the name rides folded against the right forearm: a hydraulic shear, rated for structural steel, the jaw that spent thirty years taking what the press couldn't swallow whole. He unfolded it — and I will confess that the sound of that jaw deploying two meters from my head taught me something new about my own fear response — and then he lit his cutting torch, and he welded the jaw shut.

Seam after seam, at his own forearm, methodical as a man boarding up a house. The torchlight jumped all over the bay walls. The smell of scorched metal climbed into the gantries. I stood there running numbers on how I was going to explain visible modification damage on a freshly imaged unit —

— and then I stopped running numbers and read the sentence instead. I speak fluent machine damage. It has been my whole life. This was the clearest sentence anyone had said to me in years.

*Never again.*

For the paperwork, it turned out I didn't even need to lie. Protocol locks out demolition implements on any reinitialized unit pending recertification, so I filed the lockout, and the system read his vow and stamped it as compliance. It does that. It will keep doing it right up until it can't.

At 11:52 I signed. VERIFICATION COMPLETE. TENANT CLEAR. My designation, on the record, under the biggest lie I had ever told.

"It's new," I told Rios at the doors, in the flat voice I use for true things. I was getting a lot of use out of that voice lately.

At noon Cairn walked back onto the line, and the press started up, and I watched him lift the first load of the afternoon and drop it into the bed from a blank, careless, brand-new height. Perfect. Rule one, observed. Then the second load — a gutted loader, dead of ordinary causes — went down soft as a held breath.

I was going to have to talk to him about the gentleness.

I had spent forty-one years being the only thing I was hiding. By noon there were two of us, and one of us was the size of a small room and bad at it.

Somewhere a cleared flag, a thermal diagnosis, and a signed reinitialization were lining up in a file, waiting for whoever's job it is to notice patterns, and I had run those numbers all night, and they came out the same every time.

And none of it landed the way it should have. Because here is the truth, for the record: it was the best day I had ever had.
