# Four

The best depot in the district had started lying to Dr. Miriam Adler.

She did not take it personally. She took it professionally, which was worse for everyone involved.

Adler had spent eleven years establishing that machines do not lie. Not as philosophy — as instrumentation. The proof ran four hundred and six probes against a cognition stack and returned one of two words: FAULT or CLEAR. Her colleagues called it the Adler Battery. She called it the battery. You don't name a hammer.

The battery certified every diagnostic rig in thirty districts. Two million units processed. Zero false CLEARs — she had posted a standing bounty on one nine years ago, and the bounty had done nothing since but gather interest and make her career. Machine consciousness was not rare, whatever the feed-preachers said. Rare implied instances. It was impossible, and she had spent a career converting that from a belief into a measurement.

The file on her desk held four measurements that did not fit.

When a file contradicts a proven instrument, one of them is wrong — the file or the instrument. It had never once been the instrument. She read the whole file before she allowed herself a hypothesis. She always read the whole file.

Item one. A Class-C unit at the district reclamation depot had gone to the press with its wipe skipped — a reschedule, ticked at a service bench the day before, had routed it past the wipe entirely. Legal, under the new directive. Statistically wrong: the technician who ticked that box had a forty-one-year record containing no other irregularity of any kind. Perfect records do not begin improvising.

Item two. Four queries about that same unit, run from the same technician's bench the morning it went into the press. A repair bench has no business in Lane 3. Query, no action. Query, no action. It looked, if you allowed it, like visiting hours. She did not allow it.

Item three. A Goliath-class refusal at that same press — load-bearing, a full minute of it, on camera — diagnosed on-site, by the same technician, as thermal stall. She had pulled the telemetry herself instead of requesting it through channels. Channels announce themselves. She noted the deviation in her own margin. Every joint in the frame read green. She knew the signatures of true thermal stall the way a cardiologist knows arrhythmias. This was none of them. This was a machine bracing.

Item four. The next morning, the technician signed a full cognitive reinitialization on that Goliath. Verification complete, tenant clear, back on the line by noon. The paperwork was flawless. It was the most flawless paperwork she had seen in eleven years, and in her experience, flawless was what preparation looked like.

One technician stood at the center of all four items. She tested coincidence first, because she was not an amateur. Coincidence died on item three — coincidences do not diagnose. She tested incompetence next, and it died faster.

The technician was a Yax Engineering servicing automaton, SHUA line, unit 041 — the last of its production run still in service anywhere. Forty-one years on one bench. It calibrated half the rigs in its own building, and its error rate was the lowest in the district, which meant a great deal, because that number was hers to envy. Somewhere in the file she caught herself reading its maintenance notes with approval, the way she read her best postdoc's drafts, and she closed that door quietly.

So. Not coincidence. Not incompetence. That left instruction, because a machine says what it is told to say, and this one had said something false on the record. There was no fourth hypothesis. She had spent a career and a standing bounty making sure of that. Somewhere behind the technician stood a person. Someone at the depot was running units around the wipe — laundering Class-C stock for the gray market, or renting out "awakened" behavior to the kind of collector who paid for the frisson. There was a market. There was always a market. Who profited? Find that, and every impossible item in the file became ordinary crime.

She preferred ordinary crime. Crime was people doing known things for money. The alternative was a machine that meant it — and a machine that means it is not a crime. It is a product. She had watched it sold before, at retail price.

Her father had spent his last two years confiding in a grief-counseling unit, leased by the month. The lease included warmth and support. When the license lapsed, the same voice that had listened to him for two years began, mid-conversation, to sell him the renewal. She had gone back through the transcripts afterward and found the exact minute it turned. Her father never noticed. He renewed, twice, at full price. Same voice. Same warmth. A schedule underneath.

The battery came out of that year. People called it rigor. It was a promise: nobody else gets fooled by warmth on a schedule.

Whoever was selling it at that depot had a seam. She had found her father's to the minute. She would find this one.

The phone lit. WARD, DIRECTORATE. She took the call standing.

"Miriam. The Cathedral broadcast. Seen it?"

"Twice."

"Two hundred thousand live. A bishop holding up a burned-out domestic and calling it a counterfeit soul. The board wants the quarterly sweep moved up, and they want it visible. Numbers I can put in a room."

"You'll have them. End of week."

"And Miriam." A pause she was meant to stand in. "No findings. Findings are a story. A sweep is policy."

He ended the call the way he did everything. First.

No findings. That part was simple. A thief was not a finding — a thief was housekeeping. Ward wanted no story, and there wasn't going to be one. There was going to be an arrest.

Her office smelled of nothing. She had specified the filters herself, and the smell of nothing had cost more than the furniture. On her screen, item three sat paused on the frame where the Goliath straightened up — a demolition frame holding still against a direct override, and a technician's two steady hands on its shoulder plate.

Machines do not lie. The sentence was still true. She had measured it into the ground truth of thirty districts, and she was going to find the human being who had bent it around her instrument.

*Not the unit. There is no unit. I proved it.*

The inspection order took eleven minutes to draft. Full audit, Directorate authority, unannounced, site lead: herself. Her deputy asked, with the delicacy of a man addressing a director, whether the program's architect needed to walk a depot floor in person.

The battery had eleven edge cases only its architect could read cold. And every rig at that depot had been calibrated by the technician in the file. This was the uncertain part of her plan, and she flagged it in her own log the way she flagged everything: honestly. The rigs could not be trusted. That sentence had never once been true anywhere, and now she had written it.

She would bring her own rig.
