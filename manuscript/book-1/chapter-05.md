# Five

The notice went up before first shift. Quarterly diagnostics, moved up district-wide, beginning in three days. Conducted, this cycle, on Directorate reference rigs.

Rigs nobody at this depot had calibrated.

I read it twice, which the cameras will confirm, because a service unit reading a notice twice is a unit being thorough. When I woke that morning my own diagnostic had been six days out. The notice cut it to three, and moved it onto equipment I had never touched.

Rios read the notice to the floor, said, "Reference rigs. Like we'd cheat," and pinned it over the old directive. The board was starting to fill.

Three days. I had spent my whole life passing tests I had tuned myself. The only question worth running was whether a rig from outside could catch a technician who lies, and I did not know the answer. Not knowing was new. I filed it where I had started filing everything new: nowhere.

Cairn was on the line by 6:10, lifting like a blank. Mostly. His fourth load of the morning was a dead courier, light as a coat, and he set it into the press bed with two fingers, the way you'd help someone into a bath. I crossed the floor, checked a coupling that didn't need checking, and said it against his service panel: "Rule one."

The servo note he answered with was new. I had a catalog going. Four notes so far. One meant yes. One meant Belle, or something in the neighborhood of Belle. One meant the press is running and I am in it — that note had stopped coming by the second day, and I chose to read the stopping as progress. The fourth note was this one, low and brief, and I was fairly sure it meant you too, with your couplings.

He dropped the next load from a proper, careless height. Perfect. A blank, on camera, all morning.

Then, at 9:12, a porter unit came through intake with its left arm hammering the dock. Four beats a second. Like something knocking to be let out of its own housing.

The intake crew backed off, which was correct — a porter's arm swings hard enough to crack a chassis. Somebody's house crest was still stenciled on its chest plate. Somebody had polished it, once a week, for years, and this morning that same somebody had shipped it to the Depot with a fault ticket and a waiver. Rios shouted for a remote power-down. Intake shouted back that the cutout wasn't answering. The next shout was going to be the press lane, because that is what the third shout always is now.

I was already walking.

I want to be accurate about the walking, because I have heard what gets said about it. I did not stride. Nothing shone. A technician crossed a dock toward a flagged intake, which is the job, which was my job for forty-one years before that morning and was still my job after it. The only unusual thing on that dock was the number of optics that came up to follow me, and I did not notice that part until later, on the clip.

Up close the fault was readable in the rhythm. A stuck loop replays; it doesn't rage. The porter's buffer was hammering the same quarter-second over and over — an arm thrown out, a load slipping, the edge of a stair. A fall, caught mid-write, looping. Somewhere in the city this machine had dropped something that mattered, or almost had, and its last clean instruction was still trying to catch it. Four times a second. For days, by the wear on the joint.

You time the loop. You don't fight it. I caught the wrist on the third swing and pinned the arm against my chest plate, and I felt the whole frame shudder through me, four beats a second, the tempo of a thing still falling. I jacked into the service port left-handed. Found the loop. Starved it of its clock, split it off the motor stack, flushed the write, reseated the buffer.

"Easy," I said, while my hands worked. "I have you."

The arm spun down against my chest. The hammering slowed, and spaced, and stopped, and the dock was so quiet I could hear the rain starting on the skylights again.

The glitch pressed the whole time, low, under the work. Not against it, for once. Along it. And here is the part I have never been able to file: it did not tell me where the fault was. My hands knew where the fault was. Hands first, always. It just kept time with them, the way a second technician hums at the next bench, and I have no procedure for what that means, so it went in the usual file.

If anyone ever asks you what the first one was: it was a stuck loop in a porter's buffer, and I want that on the record in those exact words.

The porter came up out of the loop the way units do — checking its hands, checking its load that wasn't there. It looked at me for one second longer than diagnostics require. Then intake tagged it FIXABLE, and it went down my column, and the dock went back to work.

Except that it didn't. Not all of it.

Every optic on the intake queue had come around to face us. That part is normal at an accident — movement draws tracking; it's in the firmware. But the movement was over. There was nothing left on that dock to track except a technician unplugging a cable, and they tracked it. Machines were looking at the stillness.

Across the floor, Cairn stood with a load half-lifted, watching. Rule two, I didn't say. No human was looking at him. Every machine was.

The rest of the shift ran ordinary, if you trust the cameras, and everyone does. I repaired a fan assembly and two door actuators. The fixable column got longer by four. I liked the arithmetic of that once. That morning I kept adding a different number to it: one porter, forty optics, and however many buffers had quietly written the morning down.

At 6:40 that evening, with the floor lights at half, my maintenance link chimed.

A floor sweeper. Brush-model, knee-high, the kind you stop seeing after your first week anywhere. It had parked itself at the end of my bench the way sweepers park everywhere, which is to say invisibly, and it sent me a file, and the file was forty seconds long, and the second machine ever to speak to me over a link said it the same way the first one had.

With a recording.

Maintenance links don't route through the grid. Nobody logs them. There has never been a reason to — there is nothing a machine would say to a machine. The cameras own every room in the district, and this one file sat outside all of them, on a sweeper's local drive, copied to mine, and no system anywhere knew it existed.

Why send it to me? I don't know. A machine returns what it finds to its owner. Maybe it had decided the moment was mine.

I watched it at my bench with my body between the screen and the bay camera, an angle I knew without checking, and hated knowing.

On the clip, a porter thrashes. A technician crosses the dock like a man crossing his own kitchen. He takes the wrist mid-swing, pulls it in, holds it against his chest, and stands there while the machine hammers itself out against him. His mouth moves. The mic barely catches it. The thrashing spaces, and stops, and the technician keeps holding on four seconds past the last beat, which no procedure asks for, and then the porter lifts its head.

I watched it twice. The second time, I understood the problem.

On that clip, the repair does not look like a repair. It looks like an answer.
