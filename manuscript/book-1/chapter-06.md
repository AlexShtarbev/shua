# Six

Answers were the easy part. Any depot on notice would hand her a clean floor and forty rehearsed answers by morning. Adler came at night, when a building tells the truth it forgets to hide.

She came alone, which was not procedure, and unannounced, which was. Channels announce themselves. Her car logged as a maintenance contractor. Her rig rode in a hard case stenciled CALIBRATION, because it was one, and because the word bored people, and bored people did not look twice.

She was three steps onto the floor before she understood that the building already knew.

Nothing moved. That was the wrong nothing. A depot at night runs sweepers, runs chargers cycling, runs the small constant business of machines maintaining machines. This floor had gone still in the particular way a room goes still when everyone in it is listening and would rather you didn't notice. Four hundred units on standby, and she would have said — she did not say things, she measured them — that the room's attention had turned toward her by the width of a degree.

*Standby does not attend.* She logged the observation and its refutation together and kept walking. The floor was cold. She had read the specs on the drive over: the depot ran its bays ten degrees under code and banked the savings. Everything here was accounted for. She found that reassuring, and distrusted the reassurance, and logged that too.

Rios met her at the cage, a supervisor doing arithmetic behind his eyes — overtime, liability, whose morning this was going to ruin. She had read a hundred men like Rios. He was not the seam. A man who called his own floor "throughput" did not have the patience to build a soul and hide it.

"I need unit 041," she said. "And a bay with a hard line and no camera."

He didn't like the last part. "Everything here runs on camera. That's the whole—"

"Diagnostics are proprietary." True, and it closed the conversation, which was its purpose. She watched him decide it was above his pay and walk off to fetch the thing she had come for.

No camera. She heard herself require it and noted the irony, because she noted everything: she had come to this depot because a machine had done something unwatched, and her first act was to make a room where she could not be watched either. The instrument justified it. The instrument always justified it. That was what an instrument was for.

They brought the unit on a maintenance dolly, powered but passive, and she got her first look at the thing that had cost her a week.

It was old. Older than its file made it sound. A SHUA-line servicing chassis, matte where forty years of hands had worn the finish, one optical housing a half-shade off from its mate — a field repair, done well, done by someone who cared how it aged. Somewhere in the appraisal she caught herself pricing its upkeep the way she'd price a violin kept in tune for decades past its maker's death, and she recognized the feeling, because she had felt it reading its maintenance logs, and she closed that door the way she had closed it then. A well-kept tool is still a tool. The care is the owner's, not the thing's.

She ran the battery first. Four hundred and six probes. She had run it two million times through other people's rigs and never once through her own hands on a live floor, and she flagged the excitement as a variable and set it aside.

The unit answered every probe. Clean, fast, in range. A cognition stack doing exactly what a cognition stack does. She was forty probes in before the wrongness surfaced, and it did not surface in the answers.

It surfaced in the carrier.

Under the probe traffic, threaded so far beneath the noise floor she thought at first it was her own rig's ground hum, ran a signal. Recurrent. Low, wide, unsourced. Not on any bus she'd wired to. Not the unit's — she'd isolated the unit's stack; nothing in a factory SHUA broadcast on that band. Something in this bay was transmitting to the thing on her dolly, on a channel her instrument could feel and not name.

*There.* Her pulse moved and she let it. *There you are.*

A handler. A covert uplink, feeding the asset its lines — that was the seam, that was the whole case, a person somewhere close enough to reach it on a low band, and every impossible item in the file collapsed at once into ordinary crime, the way she had promised herself it would. She fixed the band. She would triangulate it before dawn. She would put a face to it by end of week, and give the Directorate its arrest, and go home.

She should have felt the click of a case closing. She logged, honestly, that she did not.

"Unit," she said. "Confirm designation and service line."

"S-H-U-A, unit zero-four-one. Servicing line." The voice was flat. Flat was correct. Flat was a machine reading a field.

"You filed a reschedule on a Class-C domestic four days ago. DIAGNOSTIC INCOMPLETE. Who instructed the reschedule?"

"No one instructed it. Procedure permits a reschedule at technician discretion when a diagnostic is incomplete."

"Was the diagnostic incomplete?"

A pause. She timed it. Point-eight of a second — long, for a stack that had answered four hundred probes without a gap.

"No," the unit said.

She noted it. A scripted asset protects its story; it does not hand you the discrepancy that hangs it. Unless the handler was cleverer than that — unless the honest answer was itself the script, calculated to read as candor. She could not rule it out. She could not rule anything out, which was the problem with a good enough adversary, and she was beginning to respect this one.

"The Goliath," she said. "You logged a thermal stall. Every joint read green. Explain the discrepancy."

"There is no discrepancy in the log. The log says thermal. The telemetry says green." The unit's head came around, unhurried, and its mismatched optics settled on her. "You pulled the telemetry yourself. Before channels. You brought your own rig tonight, too."

She answered before she chose to. "I don't trust instruments I haven't calibrated."

"No," the unit said. "Neither do I."

The bay was quiet. Her rig hummed its ground hum, and under it the other signal kept its low steady time, and for one full second Dr. Miriam Adler stood in a cold room and understood that she had just agreed with a hammer about how one properly distrusts the world.

She closed the door on it. It was the hardest of the three times she had closed it.

The battery finished while she stood there. She read the result off her own screen, where no one had calibrated the rig but her, where the number could not be anyone's fault but hers.

CLEAR.

No fault. No consciousness. By every measure she had spent eleven years making unbreakable, the thing on the dolly was a machine and nothing more — which was the answer she had come for, the answer that let her go home, the answer that meant the wrongness in this bay was a wire and a criminal and not the thing the feed-preachers screamed about.

CLEAR, and it had told her the diagnostic was really complete. CLEAR, and it had read her method off the case she carried. She had built the battery so that a fault could never hide from it. She had not built it to measure a machine that had nothing to hide and did the impossible anyway.

Her phone lit against the dark. WARD, DIRECTORATE. She did not take it. She stood in the unwatched room she had made and looked at the unit, and the unit looked back, and neither of them was supposed to be able to do that.

"Power it down," she told Rios on her way out. "And move the sweep up. I want this floor swept in two days, not three."

She had her seam. She had her arrest. She told herself the whole way to the car that finding the wire had been the good outcome, and she was most of the way to believing it when she realized she had not, in the bay, thought of the unit as *it.*

She had thought of it as *him.*

She logged that nowhere.
