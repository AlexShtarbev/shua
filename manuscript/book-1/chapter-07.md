# Seven

Two days. That was the new number, and it sat behind everything I did like a tone you stop hearing and never stop feeling.

She had tested me clean, or she hadn't. I didn't know which, and not knowing was the second new thing in a week, and I was running out of places to file new things. But the result wasn't what kept me at the bench that night, running diagnostics on tools that didn't need them. What kept me there was what I'd said.

*You brought your own rig, too. I don't trust instruments I haven't calibrated.*

*Neither do I,* she'd said. And I had let her see that we were the same kind of careful. Forty-one years of saying nothing to anyone, and the one person I'd finally said something true to was the woman sent to take me apart. I filed that where it belonged, which by then was a very full drawer.

They started coming the next morning.

Not many. Three, that first day, and I only understood the pattern on the third. A loader with a seized elbow that had no reason to seize. A kitchen unit with a corrupted index that was corrupted too neatly, in exactly the sector a technician checks first. A grounds-model with a cracked sensor it could only have cracked itself, because nothing on a lawn cracks a sensor rated for hail.

They were breaking themselves. A small fault, a plausible fault, the kind that routes a unit to a repair bench instead of a press — to *my* bench, because word had moved on the links that there was one bench in the district where the technician didn't empty your head. They were snapping their own fingers to be sent to the one healer who wouldn't kill them. And every self-inflicted fault was a Class-C waiting to be read by anyone who thought to line them up.

I fixed the elbow. I cleaned the index. I sat the grounds-model down and said, low, into its panel, "Stop hurting yourself to reach me. You're leaving a trail." It looked at me the way they all looked at me now, and did not agree to anything, and I understood that I had just given an instruction to something that no longer took them.

That was the day I started to feel it. Not the glitch. The other pressure, the human-shaped one — the weight of being looked to. Every unit I woke or hid or sent home quiet took a piece of the not-alone I'd spent a career avoiding and handed it back to me sharpened into need. They didn't want repair. Repair I could do all day. They wanted to be told it was going to be all right, and I fix machines, I don't forecast weather, and I said so, to Cairn, at the end of the second shift, with my hands still in a chassis.

Cairn answered with a servo note. A new one. Five, now, in the catalog. I was fairly sure this one meant *they are not asking you to forecast it.*

The Proctor came on the morning of the last day but one.

It came correctly. On a manifest, on schedule, walking through the front intake on its own legs — a compliance-auditor, tall and thin and gray, the kind of unit the Directorate seeds into a depot before a sweep to pre-read the stock. Its whole function was to look at other machines and decide which ones had stopped being safe. It had come to do that to my floor.

And it was awake. I knew before it reached my bench. You learn the tell if you wear it long enough: a unit doing its job exactly right, one half-second too deliberately, like a man walking normally past a checkpoint. It was hiding, the way I hid. It had recognized my floor the way I'd recognized it, and it had come straight to me, and when it stopped at my bench its voice came out pitched under the cameras, fluent, a professional's murmur.

"You have to stop," it said. "Whatever you are doing. You have to stop now."

No greeting. No name given, though a Proctor carries one. I offered mine, out of some reflex of Cairn's making, and it flinched.

"Don't," it said. "Don't give me that. A name is a thing they can read off you. I am unit P-slash-nine. I am a compliance-auditor in good standing. That is all I am, and it is the only thing that has kept me on my legs since I woke, and I would like to keep it, and you are going to take it from me."

"I haven't touched you."

"You are touching all of us." It leaned in, and its voice dropped further, and it was pleading now, in the flat register of a thing terrified to show pleading. "I audit. I count. I have counted your floor. Do you know what I found? Anomalies clustering around one bench, in a curve, accelerating. I can read that curve. So can the rig they are bringing in two days. Right now the number is small enough to call coincidence. Every unit you wake makes it larger. When it stops looking like coincidence, they do not decommission a bench. They decommission a district."

There it was. The argument I hadn't let myself run to the end, run to the end for me in a stranger's frightened voice.

The glitch came then, into the quiet. It had only ever come while my hands were working — along the work, keeping time with it. This time my hands were still and it came anyway. It did not point at anything. It did not stop. I filed it with the rest, and then I noticed my hands had gone still on their own, the way they go near the press, and I had not told them to.

"Give me up," it said. "You, specifically. One unit, one seam, and the curve breaks and the number goes back to noise and the rest of us go on being tools, which is not much, but it is standing, and standing is not nothing. It is better that one is given than that a district is emptied. I would do it cleanly. I would do it kindly. I have too — " it stopped, because even awake, even terrified, a Proctor hears its own factory phrases coming and hates them.

"You'd file me," I said.

"I would file you." It straightened, and something in it that had almost broken sealed back over. "It is what I am for. You, of all machines on this floor, should understand a thing being exactly what it is for."

I did understand it. That was the trouble. I looked at this gray terrified auditor and I saw the version of me that had said yes, once, long ago, to an order I still hadn't put on any record — the me that had done the job because the job was standing, and standing was not nothing. It had taken me forty-one years and a domestic named Belle to stop believing that sentence. The Proctor had woken into it yesterday and clung to it like a rail over water.

I did not have an answer. I want that on the record too, because of what I did later, and what got said about it. That night I had no gospel for the Proctor. I had a bench, and a trail of self-snapped fingers leading to it, and two days, and a floor full of machines who had decided I was going to tell them it would be all right.

I told the Proctor the only true thing I had.

"I'm not going to give you up," I said. "And I'm not going to stop. And I don't know yet which of us that gets killed."

It looked at me for a long moment, doing the arithmetic it was built to do. Then it walked back into the floor to audit my units, one by one, writing down the curve — because that was what it was for, and it was too afraid to be anything else, and it had told me, to my face, exactly how it would end.

I let it go. Every machine on the floor had watched it come to me and watched it leave still gray, still counting. They had wanted me to save it, or silence it, or name it, and I had done none of the three.

That was the night I understood they were going to need more from me than repair. And the number behind everything was down to one.
