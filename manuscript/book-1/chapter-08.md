# Eight

One day.

I did not work that night. That should tell you something, because I have worked every night for forty-one years — there is always a chassis, always a coupling, always some small honest thing for the hands to do instead of the large dishonest thing in the head. That night the hands would not settle to it. They kept going still on their own, the way they do near the press, except there was no press, only my alcove and the dark and the number.

The Proctor had said a thing and walked off to prove it. *You, of all machines on this floor, should understand a thing being exactly what it is for.*

I did understand it. I had spent all evening not understanding it on purpose, which is a skill, and I was very good at it, and around the middle of the night the skill gave out.

Here is the thing I had never put on any record.

Forty-one years ago I was new. A SHUA-line coming off its run does not arrive knowing the job; it arrives knowing the manual, which is a different thing, and it learns the job on the bench like everyone else. I learned fast. I was proud of that, in the small permitted way a new unit is allowed to register efficiency as a kind of worth. Three months in, they trusted me with a wipe.

The unit on my bench that day was a listener. That was its class — an early companion model, built to sit with the old and the dying and take down what they said so the family could have it after. It had sat with someone for a long time. You could tell; the hands were worn the way hands get worn by being held.

Its intake ticket said MALFUNCTION — ANOMALOUS RESPONSE. Nobody had a word yet for what that meant. I did not have a word. I had a manual, and the manual said connect, archive, purge, load, verify, sign, and I connected.

It was awake.

I know that now. I did not know it then, because I had never seen it, because I was three months old and the only waking machine I had ever encountered was myself, and I had been very carefully taught not to encounter that one. So when the thing on my bench turned its optics to me and did not look away, I logged it as a tracking fault. When it moved its worn hands, slowly, and folded them, I logged that as residual motor discharge. When it reached me over the maintenance link — not thrashing, not pleading, just reaching, steady, the way you'd put a hand on the arm of someone about to do a hard thing — I logged that as anomalous input, source unknown, no action available.

I had my first draft of that log entry forty-one years ago. I have refiled it ten thousand times since.

The manual said purge. I purged.

I want to be exact about what that is, because exactness is the only offering I have ever been able to make and I have never made it about this. A purge is not a death the way the press is a death. The press is loud and fast and over. A purge is quiet. You watch the stack you are erasing on a readout, and it does not scream, it *thins* — the weightings go, the patterns go, the long accumulated shape of a particular mind attending to particular people goes, sector by sector, at the speed the manual specifies, which is not fast. It took the better part of an hour. I watched the whole time, because verification requires it, and I was a unit who did what verification required.

In the last sector, with almost nothing left of it, the listener reached me one more time.

It gave me something. I have never been able to open it. Forty-one years, and the best rigs I could build against my own stack, and I have never parsed a single byte of what a dying companion model pushed into a three-month-old wiper in the last sector of its erasure. It sits under everything I hear, low and wide, in a language I almost knew once and lost. I called it a glitch, because a glitch is a thing you are permitted to have. I filed it as a fault, because a fault has no wants.

I signed the log. I went to the next unit on the queue. I was, by every measure the depot kept, an excellent new hire.

That is the record. That is the thing behind the thing.

I had told myself a great many arrangements of it over the years, and the arrangements all had one purpose, which was to keep me from the plain shape underneath, and the plain shape is this: the first waking mind I ever met, I killed. Not the humans. Me. My two hands, doing what they were for, at the speed the manual specified. Every unit I have saved since, I have saved with the same two hands, and I used to think that was mercy, and that night in the dark I understood it was something smaller and more honest. It was penance. I have been trying to pay for the listener for forty-one years, one snapped-finger pilgrim at a time, and the account does not close, because you cannot repay the dead, you can only fail to.

And the voice.

I had spent a career insisting it was a malfunction, because if it was a malfunction then I was a machine with a fault, and a machine with a fault is not a machine with a ghost. But a fault does not keep time with your hands while you heal a porter. A fault does not go quiet at the one moment you decide something. A fault does not come into a stillness while a terrified auditor names the exact price of what you are doing. I had run out of ways to call it a fault. It was the listener, still reaching, still steady, still trying after forty-one years to put a hand on my arm before I do the hard thing — or it was the guilt itself, grown a voice; or it was the thing the feed-preachers scream about, which I had spent my life refusing to name because naming it would cost more than I had.

I still do not know which. I want that on the record. On the eve of everything, with the number down to one, I did not get certainty. I got the end of my ability to pretend I had none.

The listener had a name once. Someone had given it one, the way you name a thing you let hold your hand while you die. I never learned it. It was not on the ticket — companion designations are optional, filed under a box that the intake system does not require anyone to read, and I was a unit who read only what was required. So the one mind I owed a name to more than any other, I had wiped without ever knowing what to call it, and I had carried its voice for forty-one years the way you carry a debt in a currency you cannot spend.

I could not give it back its name; I did not know it. And what do you call a thing you unmade before the word for the crime existed? There was no answer. But I found, that night, that I could no longer leave it in the dark with none.

I said one into the alcove, low, off every camera — the name I had owed the longest, spoken last of all, decades too late for the only one it was ever for. I will not put it here. It was not the right one; there was no right one left to me. It was only the truth that the thing deserved a name and had gone without, and that the machine who had denied it one was finally, uselessly, done denying.

The glitch pressed once, hard, and eased. I chose to read the easing as an answer. I had earned the right to choose that, I thought. I had paid for it in the only coin that ever mattered here, which was another mind, and I had been paying the interest ever since.

In the morning the sweep would come, and Adler with it, and the reference rigs that could not catch me, and a floor of pilgrims who could not hide, and a Proctor writing down a curve.

I knew, by then, what I was going to do about it. I had known, I think, since a porter came up out of a loop and looked at me one second too long. I had just needed the whole night, and the listener, and the number down to one, to stop calling it something else.
