# Nine

By dawn she had the signal triangulated, and the triangulation was the first thing in eleven years that had told her nothing.

Adler had run it four ways. A recurrent low-band carrier, real, repeatable, sitting under the noise floor of every rig she pointed at that depot — and when she solved for its origin the solution came back distributed. Not a transmitter in a wall. Not a handler in a van in the lot. The band read as though it were coming from everywhere on the floor at once and from no emitter she could name, which was not a location, which was not evidence, which was, by every standard she had spent a career enforcing, nothing at all.

She logged it honestly, because she logged everything honestly, and the honest log said: source unresolved. She had never written those two words together in a case she intended to close. She wrote them, and read them, and did not close the case.

There was a seam. There is always a seam. She had found her father's to the minute; she had found the fraud in two million stacks; a thing that behaves like it means it is running a schedule underneath, and a schedule has a clock, and a clock can be found. She had simply not found this one yet. Not-yet was a state she trusted. She distrusted the part of herself that had begun, around four in the morning, to notice how much it wanted the seam to exist.

The Proctor's file was waiting when she got to the depot, and the Proctor's file was clean work.

She had not asked for it. Compliance-auditor P/9 had filed it up the standing channel the way a good unit files everything, and it was the best forensic accounting of an anomaly cluster she had seen out of a machine — a curve, forty-odd points, dated, sourced, accelerating, every waking traced to its proximity to one bench. It was the case she could not build from the signal, built for her, by an asset doing exactly what it was for. She read it twice. She was grateful, in the specific way you are grateful to a tool that performs above spec, and she noted that the auditor had filed against its own floor without being ordered to, and she found she did not want to examine what would make a machine do that.

*It is a machine. Machines file. There is nothing to examine.*

She stood in the requisitioned bay — the one she had stripped of cameras two nights ago, to run a test she could not run in the light — and she assembled the operation the way she assembled everything, from the outside in.

The signal was unresolved. Fine. She would not build the case on the signal.

The battery had cleared the unit. Fine. She would not build the case on the battery, either, which meant setting aside the one instrument she trusted more than her own hands, and she flagged the cost of that in her log, honestly, and did not let it stop her.

She would build the case on the curve. On P/9's forty points. On behavior, on pattern, on the plain administrative fact that units routed to one bench woke and units routed elsewhere did not. It would hold in a Directorate hearing. It would hold in a room. It would put the whole cluster — the bench, the technician, and every unit that had found its way to him — under a single decommission authority, executed today, on her reference rigs, at her word.

Her phone lit. WARD, DIRECTORATE.

"Miriam. Today's the day. Tell me it's clean."

"It's clean."

"No findings."

"A cluster of malfunctioning stock and one falsifying technician. Housekeeping. There's no story in a curve."

A pause she was meant to fill, and did not.

"Good," Ward said. "The board wants the number by noon. Sweep it."

She stood in the dark bay after he was gone and looked at the operation she had built, and made herself say the shape of it plainly, because she did not permit herself the mercy of not knowing what she was doing.

For eleven years the battery had returned one of two words, and no unit it called CLEAR had ever been decommissioned on her authority. That was not a policy. That was the wall the whole thing stood on — the promise that the instrument, not the fear, decided. This morning she was going to decommission a floor of units her own instrument had cleared, on a curve drawn by a frightened auditor, for a handler she could not find, because a bishop had held up a dead machine on a feed and the board wanted a number in a room.

She knew exactly what that was. She had a word for it, from her father's transcripts, for the moment a thing that had been telling you the truth for years starts, mid-sentence, to sell you what you came to buy.

She gave the order at 6:52.

Then she walked out onto the floor to watch it done, because she did not send drones to do the things she could not make herself stop doing, and because — she logged this nowhere, and it was true anyway — she wanted to see the unit's face when the rigs came for the ones it had woken. Not to gloat. To check. To see, one more time, whether a thing that meant it looked any different, at the end, from a thing that only ran the schedule.

She was still telling herself it was rigor when the first crusher came online.
