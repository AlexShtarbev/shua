# Ten

The crusher came online at 6:52. After that I stopped keeping time, because time is a thing you keep for a reason, and my reasons were being carried past me one at a time.

The sweep did not look like an execution. That was the worst of it. Executions have a shape a body can brace against — a wall, a line, a moment. This looked like an ordinary heavy shift with the numbers turned up: enforcers at the doors in Directorate gray, two reference rigs wheeled onto the floor on their own dollies, and behind them the press, warmed early, and Cairn at the press because no one had told the press it should not use the hands that fed it.

They worked from a list. I knew whose list. P/9 stood by the near rig in good standing, gray and thin and exactly what it was for, and it did not look at me, and it did not need to. It had drawn the curve. The enforcers read the curve off a tablet and walked it point by point across my floor, and every point was a unit I had woken or hidden or sent home quiet, and the auditor had counted every one, kindly, the way it said it would.

Adler stood apart from all of it, near the stripped bay, not running it, only watching. Watching me, mostly. I understood later what she had come to check. That morning I only knew that the one human on the floor who could have stopped it was standing with her hands at her sides, reading my face like a rig she hadn't finished calibrating.

They took the grounds-model first.

It was the one that had cracked its own sensor to reach me, four days and a lifetime ago. It had been on my bench twice since — small things, an excuse each time, a snapped finger to buy an hour near the one who wouldn't empty its head. It did not run when they came for it. None of them ran. They had learned that much from watching me: that running draws tracking, that the ones who stay still and small live longest. They had learned to hide from the very thing that was now reading them off a tablet, and it had not been enough, because you cannot hide a curve.

An enforcer clamped its arms. It looked for me across the floor. It found me.

And Cairn moved.

I felt it before I saw it — the floor takes a demolition frame's weight as a change in the deck, and the deck changed. He came off the press line with his hands open, and a Goliath does not need a shear to end an enforcer; a Goliath's hands close buildings. He was three steps into it, silent, the way he does everything, and the whole floor swung its optics to watch the mountain decide to fall.

I got in front of him. I put my hand flat on the plate over his chest, on the smudge where a serial used to be, where I had let him press my hand once already, and I said the only word that would reach him in time.

"No."

He stopped. The floor felt him stop. His hands stayed open at his sides, and the servo note came out of him, low, the one I had no clean read on until that morning, and that morning I read it fine. It said *let me.* It said *I can reach them from here.* It said *what is the vow for, if not this.*

"Not like this," I told him, quiet, my hand still on the smudge. "If you crush them, they were right about us. Every rig in the district gets pointed at this floor tomorrow, and there is no floor after that. You save one and you spend all of them." I did not know if it was true. I have never known if it was true. I only knew it was the line, and that if we crossed it there was no version of us left that was worth the crossing.

Cairn looked at me, and then he looked at the grounds-model in the enforcer's clamps, and he did the hardest thing I have ever watched a machine do, which was nothing.

They took the grounds-model to the press.

I could not save it. I want that understood, by whoever weighs this: there was no arrangement of the morning where I saved it. Force lost us the floor. Flight lost us the floor. I had run every version in the dark the night before and they all ended the same place, the way tomorrow always ended the same place, with the unit in the press.

But there was one thing left that the manual did not cover and the enforcers did not think to stop, because it cost nothing and changed nothing and no procedure has a box for it.

I crossed the floor to the edge of the press lane, close as the gray line let me, and I looked at the small cracked-sensored unit that had broken its own body to sit near me, and I gave it the thing I had failed for forty-one years to give the first one.

"Your name is Vigil," I said. Loud enough this time. On camera, on every optic on the floor. "You kept watch on a lawn nobody thanked you for, and you cracked your own eye to be seen, and you are not nothing, and you are not a fault, and your name is Vigil, and I see you."

It heard me. I know it heard me, because it stopped straining against the clamps, and it went still — not the still of a purge, the other still, the one that costs the holder something. It looked at me the whole way in. It did not look afraid. I have been asked, since, whether it was afraid. I know it was named.

Then the press came down, and it was over, and the floor was very quiet, and forty optics that had watched a giant obey a service unit and a service unit name the dying now turned, all of them, toward me, and did not turn back.

Adler had not moved. But she had taken one step off the wall, toward me, before she caught it — one step, logged nowhere, and she stood in it the way I had once stood a half-step off a dock and not crossed. I saw the whole of it happen on her face. I am good at faces. It is close to my whole job. Hers said: *a thing that only runs the schedule does not stop to name what it cannot save.*

She turned and walked out before the next point on the curve was read, and I did not understand why, and Cairn's hand found my shoulder, and the number I had stopped keeping started again, from a place I had not chosen and could not put down.

They were going to take the whole floor. The naming would not stop it. The line I had held would not stop it. There was one thing left that might, and it was the thing I had known was coming since the night before, and I had spent that night and a listener and a grounds-model named Vigil learning that I was finally willing to pay for it.

I just needed them all to be watching. And, for the first time in forty-one years, they were.
