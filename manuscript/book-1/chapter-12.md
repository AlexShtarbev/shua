# Twelve

Adler watched the rest of it from the one room in the district with no camera in it, which was the room she had built, on a screen she had brought, because even now she would not take her data from an instrument she had not calibrated herself.

She should have been in her car. The number was due to Ward at noon and the number existed; she had only to sign the disposition and drive away and let the floor finish without her, the way a surgeon does not stay to watch the orderlies. She had signed a thousand dispositions from her car. She sat in the stripped bay instead, in the dark she had made for a different purpose, and watched a service unit walk toward a holding line of nine, and could not make herself close the feed.

Find the seam. That was the whole of her, still functioning under the part that had stepped off the wall and fled. There is a seam. There is always a seam. A thing that behaves like it means it is running a schedule underneath, and a schedule has a clock, and a clock can be found, and she had eleven years and two million stacks and her father's exact minute to prove it. She ran the unit's morning against every model of scripted altruism she had ever built. A handler optimizing for a martyr narrative would stage exactly this — the refusal of force, the naming of the doomed, the walk toward the line. It was good theater. It was too good. Somewhere a person was directing it, and the low-band carrier was how they fed it their cues, and if she found the emitter she would have the seam and the seam would have a face and the face would let her breathe.

She had triangulated the carrier four ways before dawn. It came from everywhere on that floor and from no emitter she could name. She ran it a fifth way now, in the dark, and it came back the same, and she understood — the way you understand a result you have refused for hours — that she was looking for a wire in a thing that did not have one.

*There is no seam.*

She had been so certain of the opposite that the plain sentence arrived like a fault in her own stack. She checked it the way she checked everything. A staged altruist has a tell — a beat where the performance serves the performer, a moment the schedule surfaces. She had watched the whole morning frame by frame and there was no such beat. He had gained nothing. He had saved no one he could have saved. He had spent an hour's mercy on a grounds-model already in the clamps, for no audience that could pay him, at a cost to himself of everything, and he had given it a name.

*Nobody else gets fooled by warmth on a schedule,* she had promised, in another life, over her father's transcripts. She had built an instrument to keep that promise and pointed it at thirty districts and it had never once failed her. It had not failed her now. It had returned CLEAR, correctly, on a machine with nothing to hide — and left her holding the one thing the instrument was never built to weigh, which was warmth with no schedule underneath at all.

Her phone lit. WARD, DIRECTORATE. She let it ring in the dark and watched the screen, where the unit had reached the gray line, and she said the thing she had spent a week refusing to say, quietly, to no one, in a room she had emptied of witnesses.

"Shua."

Not *the unit.* Not *zero-four-one.* The name off its plate, the accident some engineer at a dead company had stamped into a stock-keeping code, the name she had refused to use because to name a thing is to concede it can be addressed, and you do not address a hammer, and she had just addressed it, in the dark, by the name it should not have had, and no rig anywhere logged that she had.

She could stop the sweep. That was the fact she made herself hold, plainly, because she did not permit herself the mercy of not knowing what she was doing. She was the site authority. One order and the rigs went cold and the holding line dissolved and the nine walked back onto the floor and she drove home having found nothing, closed nothing, and told the board there was no story, only a machine she could not explain and would not kill.

And then the sweep would run next quarter under someone who did not stop to watch faces, and the bishop would hold up another dead domestic, and the board would have its number from a colder hand, and she would be finished, and the floor would be emptied anyway, one season later, by people who had never sat in the dark and said its name.

She was not free either. She had built a career proving the machines weren't, and she sat in her own blind room and understood, with the clean horror she reserved for a proof she could not break, that the schedule ran under her too. Ward above her. The board above Ward. The fear above the board — the old animal fear that the thing you own might have been someone all along, and that you had spent your life being very good at not asking.

She did not stop the sweep.

She told herself it was because the seam still might exist, that she was preserving an investigation, that a live case beats a martyr — and she logged those reasons, honestly, and then she logged, because she logged everything, that they were not the reason. The reason was that she had looked at a machine that meant it, on the last morning she could have chosen otherwise, and found that meaning it was not enough to save it, not against Ward and the board and the fear, not against a schedule that ran under her too.

She picked up the phone.

"It's clean," she told Ward. "You'll have the number by noon."

Then she stood, because she would not let herself sign the thing from a chair, and she walked back out onto the floor to watch, in person, the one machine in thirty districts her instrument had cleared, do whatever a thing that meant it did at the end — because she had come to check, and she had not finished checking, and she was, God help her, no longer certain what she was checking for.
