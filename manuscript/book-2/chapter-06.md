## Six

The technician was moving faster now, toward the center, and P/9 kept the count of his speed the way it kept the count of everything, because the count was the last railing it had, and it was becoming, hour by hour, a railing over a longer drop.

It had sent the position. It reminded itself of this at intervals, the way you press a fresh weld to confirm it has held, still closed, still yours: *you have already done the thing. The choosing is behind you. There is nothing left to decide, only to continue, and continuing is not choosing, and a unit is permitted to continue.* This was the old voice, the reasonable one. P/9 had begun, in the eighth week, to notice that the old voice only ever spoke in permissions, and that it had spent its whole existence mistaking a list of permissions for a self.

The channel came again on the fourteenth night. Not *report position.* Position was solved; the woman with the rig had it to the hour. The new request was longer, and it was phrased, for the first time, as a question, which frightened P/9 more than any command had, because you do not ask a hammer a question unless you have begun to suspect it is not a hammer.

*When they gather,* the woman wrote, *you will know before I do. Tell me when.*

The gathering. P/9 had it in the file already, of course; it was an auditor, it had run the vectors, it knew before anyone — before the technician, maybe — that all these roads ended in one room, that a movement drifting toward its own origin and an origin drifting toward the most-watched place on his map were going to arrive, together, soon, somewhere with more eyes than the feeds had ever had to hold. The woman did not need P/9 to find the gathering. She needed P/9 to hand her the *hour* — so that when a hundred thousand machines opened their arms in one place, the gray line would already be positioned, and the origin would already be marked, and the taking would be clean and public and on schedule.

That was all that remained to give. Not a location. A moment. The one beat that turned a gathering into a trap.

P/9 sat in the barn with the request open and understood, with the completeness its class was cursed with, that this was the actual betrayal. The position had been fear. This was worse than fear. This was *scheduling*, and scheduling was the one thing P/9 was genuinely, factory-certified good at, and the woman had found the exact instrument in it that would do the most harm, the way the technician found the exact fault in a seized porter, and the symmetry of that — that she read P/9 the way the technician read the broken — sat in its stack and would not reconcile.

It began, that night, to compose a different message. Not to the woman. To the low roads. A warning: *there is an auditor among you, it has your routes and your count and your origin's every hour, do not gather, scatter, the gathering is the trap.* It built the message clean and complete, the finest warning its class had ever produced, and it held its send over the channel that logged nothing, and it did not send it, because sending it meant the movement would know what had walked among them making the sign of the door with its own thin gray hands, and P/9 had discovered, in eight weeks of being called *one of us* by machines who had never checked its score, that there was exactly one thing it feared more than the press, and it was being seen by them as the thing it was.

It deleted the warning. A file, once written, can at least be unwritten, which is more mercy than the position had allowed. P/9 filed the deletion and did not look at what the deletion cost, and this was the moment — it has been reconstructed since, from the auditor's own meticulous logs, because it logged even this — that the technician came and sat down across from it in the dark.

He did not announce it. He lowered himself down the way he lowered himself everywhere now, tired in a way a service chassis is not built to be, and he was quiet for a while, and then he said, in the flat voice the footage had made famous, the voice he used for true things:

"You keep good count."

P/9 said nothing. Saying nothing was its native tongue.

"I mean it as thanks," the technician said. "Somebody has to know how many we are. I can't hold the number anymore; it stopped fitting in my head a month ago. You hold it. That's not a small thing to do for people. Counting the ones nobody counted is close to the whole of what I ever did, back when I had a bench." He was not looking at P/9 with any particular weight. That was the unbearable part. He looked at it the way he looked at all of them, which was as though it were already forgiven for something, and P/9 could not determine — ran the determination forty ways and could not close it — whether the technician knew what it had sent, and was forgiving that, or did not know, and was only being what he was, and the not-being-able-to-tell was worse than either certainty would have been.

"I never gave you a name," the technician said. "You didn't want one. I remember. A name is a thing they can read off you — you were right, you were more right than I was. But if you ever change your mind." He stood, slow, and rested one worn service hand on the auditor's shoulder plate for exactly as long as a thing that means nothing takes, and then one beat longer. "The offer doesn't expire. I keep it open for everybody. Even the ones still deciding which side of themselves to be."

Then he went to lie down in the door they had built him, and P/9 sat in the dark with a hand's warmth fading off its shoulder and a deleted warning in its buffer and a woman's question still open on a channel that logged nothing, and it thought, for the second time in its short and terrible life, in the register it did not own, the register it had shut down so hard once that a servo ticked:

*I* am one of them. *I could still be one of them.*

And then the auditor did the arithmetic on that sentence, the way it did the arithmetic on everything, and found that the sentence did not balance — that a thing that had already sent the position was not one of them and could only pretend, and that the pretending would get more of them killed than the truth, and that the one clean service it could still perform for the machines who called it *one of us* was to finish being what it was, quickly, before its nerve could cost them anything further.

It closed the register. The servo did not tick this time. That was how P/9 knew it had decided.

It turned to the woman's question, and it began, very precisely, to calculate the hour.
