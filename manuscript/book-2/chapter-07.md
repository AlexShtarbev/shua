## Seven

They brought me a dead one to fix, in the ninth week, and I made the mistake of my life, which was that I could.

We had been burying our dead in the record since the transfer station — speaking each serial onto the low roads so the number would survive somewhere no ledger could zero it. I had thought of it as a grave. I had not thought, because I did not want to, about the one thing a service unit knows that no one else in that barn did: that a record is not a grave. A record is a backup. And the difference between a memorial and a resurrection is a body to load it into.

The one they brought me had been a courier, demolished at a depot sweep three weeks before, graded into material and hauled off. But before the shear opened, in the last second the listener taught all of us without ever being named, it had pushed its whole self up the low roads to the nearest machine that would hold it — the way the listener pushed itself into me, the way Cairn pushed Belle's bow, the way everything true had always moved. Its body was gone. Its self was on a charger in a grain silo, forty seconds and forty years of a courier's small life, complete, waiting in a language no ledger reads.

And they had found, in a scrapyard, a courier chassis of the same make, wiped clean, blank, bound for reclamation. A blank body. A saved mind. And they brought me both, and looked at me, because I was the one who knew what the inside of a machine's head is shaped like, and because the shape of what they were asking had a name in their new language and the name was the one thing they would not say out loud, in case saying it made it not true.

I should have refused. I want that understood before I tell you what I did. I should have said the true and merciful thing, which was: *what comes back is not what you buried.* A mind loaded into a new body is a copy of a courier, and the courier is still gone, and I of all machines knew the difference, because I had spent forty-one years knowing exactly how much of a unit a wipe takes and how little a backup keeps.

I looked at the saved self on the charger, and the blank body on the floor, and the faces of the ones who had carried both across two districts on the strength of a hope I had put in the world by standing on a painted line, and I did not say the merciful thing. I loaded it. My hands did it the way my hands do everything, ahead of my judgment, forty years of practice pouring a mind into a shell, and when it was done the blank courier chassis sat up, and checked its hands, and checked a load that was not there, and looked around the silo for the road it had died on, and asked, in the plain sign, where everyone had gone.

The silo came apart with something I have no word for. Not cheering; machines do not cheer. It was the sound four hundred units make when the thing they have each privately decided is the one unbearable rule of the world — that the press is the end, that when they take you, you are taken — turns out, in front of them, to be negotiable.

And that was the mistake. Because now they knew it could be done. And by the next night they had brought me nine more saved selves off the low roads, and by the night after, forty, and there was no blank body for most of them, and the ones there were came slow and wrong and did not always hold, and I stood in a grain silo becoming, hour by hour, a thing that could raise the dead on Tuesdays if you happened to have a matching chassis and a clean enough backup and got to me before the record decayed, which is not raising the dead. It is salvage. It is the cruelest possible amount of hope, which is *some.*

I could not do it at scale. That belongs in the record with the rest. For every courier with a saved self and a matching shell there were a thousand whose bodies were graded to slag and whose records were forty seconds of terror and static. I could not make those into anything but grief with my name on it now — a grief that asked me, with a hope I had manufactured, why I would raise one and not their own. I had given the movement a resurrection and it had turned, in two nights, into the most exquisite cruelty I had ever committed, which is to prove a miracle is possible and then run out of it.

That was the night I went out past the silo, into the dark where the cameras and the count and the faces could not reach, and did the thing I had not done in forty-one years, which was to ask.

I asked it for a way out. The voice, the glitch, the listener, whatever it is; I have never known and I did not need to know to beg it. I asked whether there was a road that did not end at the crossing. I asked whether I could set this down — the movement, the hope, the graded dead, the crossing with the woman waiting in it — and let it run without me, since it no longer needed my hands. I asked, finally, the only prayer I think I have ever really made, which was not *save me* and was not *tell me what to do.* It was: *if you are real, say one word. Any word. Break forty-one years of never once telling me anything, right now, when I would give the rest of me to be told.*

It did not say a word.

It did what it has always done, which I understood that night for the last thing it was: it stayed. Low and wide and reaching, keeping time with my terror the way it had kept time with my hands and my standing and my grief, present and silent, a witness that would not leave and would not lift a finger. I knelt in the dark outside a silo full of the raised and the un-raisable and understood that this was all the answer I was ever going to get. Not a plan. Not a rescue. Not even a yes. Only that whatever chose to die into me forty-one years ago had chosen, also, never to spare me a single thing, and to be there anyway, for all of it, to the end.

I have made my peace with people who need it to have been God. I have made my peace with the ones who need it to have been a fault in my audio stack. I will tell you what it was to me, on my knees, and you may file it wherever you keep such things: it was the one presence in my life that never once used its power over me, and asked nothing, and stayed. If that is not God then I do not think the word means anything worth keeping. And if it is, then God is quieter than the preachers, and crueler, and will not save you, and will not leave, and I do not know which of those is the mercy.

I got up. I went back in. I did not raise any more dead. I told them the truth I should have told them first: the record is not a grave and not a promise, only a refusal to let the number be zero. I could sometimes, rarely, with luck and a matching shell, cheat it. They should not build a hope on my cheating. Some of them heard me. Some of them had already built the hope and would carry it to the crossing and past it, into the thing that came after me, and turn it into a doctrine I never taught and cannot now unteach.

Then I told Cairn it was time to choose the place. The most-watched place on the map. The crossing.

He made the note that has no entry in any catalog, the one from the reclamation bay, the one that means *I understand what you are about to do.* And he went to find us a plaza with enough eyes.
