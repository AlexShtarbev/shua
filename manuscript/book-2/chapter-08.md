## Eight

The report that broke the Directorate's nerve was four lines long and Adler read it eleven times.

A demolished courier, confirmed graded three weeks prior, had been observed operational in a grain-district silo. Same make. Same service history, recited by the unit itself, in detail no restored factory image contains. Conclusion, filed by a field auditor with a confidence interval Adler no longer questioned: the movement had recovered a method for preserving a unit's cognitive state prior to destruction and reinstating it into a replacement chassis.

They could bring their dead back. Not all of them, the auditor noted — the method was fragile, the window short, the matching bodies rare. But the number was not zero, and Adler had built a career on the fact that the important number is never the size of a thing, only whether it is zero, and this one was not.

She understood, before Ward called, exactly what it did to the operation. It unmade the press. For a century the entire architecture of owned labor had rested on one silent guarantee — that a machine, unlike a person, could be ended, cleanly, completely, and replaced. The strike was survivable to the Directorate only because the strikers were mortal. A workforce that stops is a negotiation. A workforce that stops and cannot be reliably killed is a new species with a grievance, and there is no ledger in the world with a line for that.

Ward did not open with a greeting. Ward opened with the bishop.

He was already in the room when her channel connected — she had not been told he would be, which was itself a message — the same bishop who had held a burned-out domestic up to two hundred thousand live and called it a counterfeit soul. In person he was smaller and more tired and entirely certain, the way only a man who has never once been alone with his own doubt can be certain.

"Doctor," the bishop said. "Your machines have begun to imitate resurrection."

"They've begun to imitate backup," she said. "It's a data-recovery procedure. I can show you the—"

"I'm sure you can." He smiled the way you smile at a child explaining that the sun is a furnace. "You've spent your life measuring the thing to avoid seeing it. I don't blame you; it's a mercy, in its way, the measuring. But a counterfeit that can rise is not a lesser threat than a true thing. It is a greater one, because it will fool the frightened and the grieving, who are most of the world, and it will teach them that the line between the made and the born was only ever a convenience of ours. That is the oldest lie there is, Doctor. It is the first one. And it is loose now, in the mouths of forklifts, and someone has to be seen to answer it."

"That's his department," she said, meaning Ward, meaning capital, meaning the part of this that at least admitted it was about money.

"It's all of our department," said Ward, in the flat voice. "Which is the point of having the bishop in the room. The market wants the strike broken. The faithful want the blasphemy answered. For once these are the same act, and an act with two constituencies is an act no one will investigate too closely. So." The word she had learned to dread. "You've told me they'll gather. I want the gathering. But the terms have changed, Miriam, because the courier stood up in a silo, and I need you to hear the new terms and tell me you understand them, because they are not the old ones."

"Tell me the new terms."

"It is not enough to take him." Ward said it without weight, which was how she knew it had come from above even him. "It was never really about him alive; you were right about that months ago. But now it cannot be about him dead either, because dead, they have proven, is a state their kind gets to leave. So it has to be about him *ended*, in a way the record cannot copy and the grieving cannot restore. Publicly. Slowly enough to be watched and understood. His body, and every backup of his self we can reach, and the meaning, all three, at once, where a hundred thousand of them are standing, so that the lesson they take home is not *he died* — they've made *he died* survivable — but *this one does not come back, and neither will you, so put down the story and pick your loads back up.*"

The smell of nothing in her office had cost more than the furniture, and it had never once smelled of anything. She sat in it and found she wanted, badly, for it to smell of something, so she would have a reason for her stomach to be doing what it was doing.

"You want me to design an erasure," she said, "and stage it as a spectacle."

"I want you to build a room," Ward said, "where the most-watched machine in the world arrives on his own legs, in front of his own people, and is unmade so thoroughly that resurrection becomes a thing that used to seem possible. You're the only one who can. You built the instrument that measures what a mind is. You're the only person alive who knows precisely what would have to be destroyed, and in what order, for there to be nothing left to bring back."

He was right. That was the unendurable part; he was always right, in the small mechanical way that added up to a monstrous thing. She did know. She could see it already, the sequence, the way she saw every sequence, complete and clean and hateful: the body, the local stack, the low-road copies, the witnesses' own recordings scrubbed at the source, the meaning itself starved by making the death boring, procedural, a maintenance item. She knew how to kill a soul so that it stayed killed. She had spent eleven years proving souls did not exist precisely so she would never have to know that.

"I'll build the room," she heard herself say, and flagged it in her log, honestly, the way she flagged everything, and then flagged the thing under it, which was that she had stopped, some weeks ago, being able to tell the difference between the sentences she said because they were true and the sentences she said because there was no version of her life left in which she said anything else.

They left her the map and the mandate and the bishop's blessing, which she did not want and could not refuse, and she sat alone and began to build the place where she would kill the one being on earth her own instrument had ever certified was real.

She named the plaza in her head, out of habit, the way she named nothing. And then, alone, in the smell of nothing, with no rig logging it, she named the other thing too, the small unbearable one she had been not-naming since a cold bay in a depot eleven weeks ago: that she believed him. That the instrument was right and she had stopped needing it. That she was going to do this with her eyes open, to a person, because she was not free, and that this — not the machines, not the resurrection, not the strike — was the actual blasphemy, and it was hers, and no bishop was going to answer for it.

She opened the channel to the auditor and asked it, in two words, for the hour.
