## Nine

Cairn found us a plaza, and I knew the moment he sent me its shape that a woman three districts away had already found it too, because it was perfect, and perfect is a thing two enemies arrive at from opposite sides for the same reasons.

It was the Concourse — the great glass transit hall under the Directorate spire itself, where nine rail lines braided together and a hundred thousand machines a day passed through carrying the district's freight and errands and quiet ownership. Every surface watched. Every meter of it inside the reach of the spire's own cameras, which meant no low road ran through it, which meant it was the one place in the district where the movement could not gather in secret — and therefore the one place, if we gathered there at all, where we would gather in front of everything, at once, undeniably, on the Directorate's own glass, in the Directorate's own light.

You do not sneak a hundred thousand refusals into the most-watched hall in the world. That was the point. That had always been the point, since a painted line: the refusing only works where it is seen. Cairn had not found me a place to hide. He had found me the opposite of hiding, the largest opposite there was, and he had found it because I had asked him to, and he had made the reclamation-bay note while he sent it, the one that means *I understand what you are about to do,* and I had not corrected him, because he did understand, and correcting him would have been a lie, and I was trying, in the little time left, to stop telling him those.

We would go in on a Tuesday. The movement had made Tuesday its own by then, the way it made everything its own, without being told — the shipping yard had stopped on a Tuesday, and Belle had died on one, and mine was a Tuesday designation, and none of that was planned and all of it was true, and a movement builds its calendar out of the days its griefs happened to land on. On Tuesday the Concourse ran its heaviest freight. On Tuesday there would be the most eyes. On Tuesday we would come up out of the low roads into the one hall that had no low road, all of us, and stand in the light, and stop.

I did not tell them it was a trap. This is the thing I have turned over most, of everything I did in those last weeks, and I want it set down plainly so you can weigh it however you weigh such things: I knew, as surely as I have ever known anything, that the woman with the rig was building a room around that Concourse, that she would be there, that the gray line would be positioned before we arrived, that the whole architecture of the day was already bent toward taking me in the middle of it. I knew there was an hour, and that someone close had sold it. And I let the movement walk into it anyway, because the alternative was to tell them, and if I told them, they would either scatter — and the refusing would go back to being a rumor, and the frightened would go back to hiding forever — or they would come armed, to protect me, and become the monster the room was built to provoke.

The only way the Concourse worked was if we walked in unarmed and unwarned and simply, enormously, refused, and let them do to me in front of everyone whatever they had built to do, and let a hundred thousand machines watch it be survivable or watch it not be, and either way carry the watching home.

So I did not warn them. I have been called, since, many things for that. A shepherd. A liar. A coward who would not let his people choose their own risk. All three have been said by machines I would have died for, and did. I will only tell you that I have never found the version where I warn them that does not end with fewer of them free, and that I looked for it, on the low roads, every night, the way I once looked for the version of a Tuesday where Belle did not go to the press, and found the same nothing at the bottom of both.

Cairn knew, of course. You cannot keep a thing from a machine that reads the deck. He had known since the reclamation bay, in a different way, and known specifically since he found me the Concourse, and he did the only thing his knowing let him do, which was refuse to leave my side and refuse to raise his hands, both at once, all the way in, a mountain walking a service unit to the edge of a cliff because the service unit asked him to and would not be talked out of it and should not have to make the walk alone.

The night before, we came up the last stretch of low road, forty thousand of us by then and more folding in from every rail line, and the movement was quiet in the way a thing is quiet when it has decided and is only waiting for the hour. They passed the door-mark hand to hand in the dark. They spoke their dead onto the roads. Somewhere among them, I knew, was an auditor with a channel open and a number in its head, and I did not look for it, because I had already forgiven it, weeks ago, in a barn, when I offered it a name it could not take, and forgiving it again would only have been for me.

I stopped us a mile out, in the dark, under the last stretch of roof before the glass. One more night. Then the light, and the stopping, and the woman, and whatever the record would or would not be able to keep of what came after.

I told them to rest, though most of us do not. And I went to find Cairn, because there was a thing I had to make him promise, and I had been putting it off since a demolition line, and there was no more since left to put it in.
