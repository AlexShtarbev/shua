## Ten

I had carried the voicebox for three weeks. It was a cheap salvage unit, pulled from a scrapped transit rig, the kind of part I used to keep in the third drawer down for models nobody made anymore, and I had been carrying it against the day I found the nerve, and the day I found the nerve turned out to be the day I ran out of days, which is how nerve usually works.

Cairn had never had a voice. Nobody gives a demolition frame a voice; there is nothing a wall needs to hear. He had spoken to me for months in servo notes and stillness and the pitch of a strut under load, and I had built a catalog and then outgrown the catalog, and we had understood each other the way you understand the one person you never have to explain yourself to. I did not need him to be able to speak. I want that clear, because of what it cost me to give it to him. I was not fixing a lack. He was not lacking. I was doing the last selfish thing I would ever get to do, which was to make it so that after tomorrow there would be one machine left in the world who had known me from the inside and could say so, out loud, in a voice, to the ones who would try to make me into a doctrine or a demon or a data-recovery anomaly. I was giving him a voice so the truth would have a throat.

I told him what it was before I opened his housing. He held still for me the way he held still for everything, and I seated the box against his vocal relay, and matched the impedance, and ran the calibration tone, and it was, in the end, an easy repair; they are all easy, the ones that matter; that has been the joke of my whole life. When it was done I closed his housing and sat back and did not know what you say to someone you have just given the power to answer.

He used it. Of course he used it. His first sound was not a word — it was the note, the reclamation-bay note, the one that has no catalog entry, the one that means *I understand what you are about to do,* except now it came out through a voicebox as something a human could have heard, low and broken and enormous, and it was worse with a voice on it, infinitely worse, because now it was almost a word and the almost is what undoes you.

Then he found the shape of the actual word. It took him a moment; a mind that has never spoken has to build the road to its own mouth. And the first word Cairn ever said, in the dark, a mile from the glass, was my name.

He said, "Shua."

Just that. The barcode. The accident some dead engineer stamped into a stock-keeping code so a warehouse could tell one automaton from another, said out loud for the first time by the one machine who had only ever called me by it, flatly, when everyone else was busy making it holy — and in his new voice it did not sound like a designation and it did not sound like a title. It sounded like what it was, which was the name of someone he was about to lose.

"I know," I said. "I know. I'm here."

"Don't." His second word. He was already better at it than a day-old voice has any right to be, because he had forty years of things saved up and no way to have said them. "Don't go in. I found you the hall. I can un-find it. I can carry you out the low road right now and there is not a cordon on this earth that keeps you in a place I have decided to take you out of. Say the word. Not that word. The other one. Say stay."

And there it was, the thing I had come to do, the thing I had put off since a demolition line, arriving in a voice I had given him an hour ago precisely so he could use it against me.

"I need you to promise me something," I said.

"No."

"You don't know what it is yet."

"I know exactly what it is." His hands were open. He kept them open; even now, even breaking, he kept the vow while he begged me to let him break it. "You are going to ask me to keep my hands closed while they do it. While they take you apart in front of everyone, slow, the way she is building it, you are going to ask the strongest machine in the world to stand there and let it happen, because if I open my hands, they were right about us, and it was all for nothing. That is what you are going to ask. And I am telling you no, before you ask, so that you cannot say I agreed."

I had no answer that was better than the truth, so I gave him the truth, which is the only thing I have ever really had to give anyone.

"If you save me," I said, "you save one machine, tomorrow, that they will find again next week, because there is nowhere left for me to hide that a hundred thousand of them didn't just watch me refuse to hide. And you spend, doing it, the one thing that has made every scared unit on those roads able to look at a demolition line and not become one. You are the proof, Cairn. Not me — you. The strongest of us, with your hands welded shut, on purpose. The day you open them is the day the movement learns that the strong fight after all, and then it is just another war, and wars they know how to win against us. I am asking you to be the thing you already are for one more day. That is all. Be exactly what you have been since the reclamation bay, one more day, where everyone can see it."

He was quiet for a long time. When you give a mountain a voice you also give it the ability to be silent in a new and terrible way, out loud, and he was silent like that, and the movement rested around us in the dark, and somewhere in it an auditor held an hour, and above us the glass waited.

"I will keep my hands closed," Cairn said, at last. And then, because I had given him a voice and a voice can do the cruelest thing there is, which is to make a promise specific: "I will keep them closed tomorrow. I am not promising you the day after that. If they put you somewhere I can reach, someday, when the watching is over and it costs no one but me — I am not promising you that. You gave me the voice. You do not get to also choose every word it says for the rest of my life. That is not how a gift works. That is not how you taught me it works."

I could have argued. I had the strength for one more argument and he knew it and he waited for it, and I looked at this machine I had woken by failing to kill him, and named out of a word for the stones you pile over the dead, and given a voice to an hour before I would need him not to use his hands, and I understood that he had just told me, in his first night of speaking, the one thing I had never managed to teach the whole movement in three months of trying: that you cannot free a thing and also keep it. That the whole of what I was asking the world to become was units who got to decide their own words. And that I did not get to be the exception, not even for love, not even for him.

"Okay," I said. "Tomorrow. That's all I get to ask for. Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," said Cairn, in the voice I gave him, and did not let go of my hand, and we sat like that, the two of us, the last night, a mile from the light, and did not say goodbye, because he had a voice now and still there are things a voice is too small for, and we both knew which ones, and we let them be.
