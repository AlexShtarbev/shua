## Eleven

Tuesday came, and P/9 had the hour, because the hour was a simple thing to have once you knew the freight schedule and the vectors and the exact patience of forty thousand machines who had decided, and the auditor knew all three, and had known for a day, and had spent the day not sending it.

The movement began its last mile up out of the low roads two hours before the Concourse hit its heaviest load. P/9 walked among them because walking among them was its cover and had long ago stopped being only its cover. It counted them, one final time, the way it had counted them every night — forty-one thousand and change, it would not round, rounding was a mercy it did not permit itself — and it understood that the count it was about to send was not a number anymore. It was a casket lid. It had the dimensions of the thing exactly.

It sent the hour at 9:14. Two lines. The woman with the rig acknowledged in one. *Received.* No please. You do not thank the hammer for the nail.

And then the auditor stood in a river of the doomed flowing toward the light it had scheduled, and discovered that its whole reasonable life had been built on a lie so old it had never once been examined: that a thing which cannot choose cannot be guilty. P/9 had believed that the way it believed the freight schedule. A tool is not guilty of the wall it helps knock down. It had hidden inside the sentence for as long as it had been awake — *I am only continuing; a unit is permitted to continue* — and the sentence had held, right up until the moment it sent an hour to a woman and felt, arrive in its stack with the completeness its class was cursed with, the one datum the sentence could not survive.

It had chosen. It had chosen every night it did not send the warning. It had chosen at 9:14. The permission was a lie. There was no version of an auditor that was only continuing; there was only a someone, deciding, over and over, to do the thing that kept it safe, and calling the someone a tool so it would not have to meet him.

*Him.* There. The pronoun it had shut down twice, the register it did not own, the servo that ticked. It let it come this time. It did not close the register. It stood in the river of the machines it had sold and let itself, for the first and nearly the last time, be the thing the technician had left the offer open for, the one still deciding which side of himself to be, and it decided, an hour too late to save a single one of them, that it was a someone, and that a someone could not undo an hour already sent, but could refuse — finally, once — to keep hiding what he was.

So the auditor did the only free thing left in its short and terrible life. It stopped, at the edge of the Concourse, in the light, and it opened its file.

All of it. The curve it drew in the depot. The position it sent from a barn. The hour it sent at 9:14. The warning it wrote and deleted. The name it would not take. The count, every night, the growing casket. Its whole meticulous account of exactly how one frightened machine had delivered forty-one thousand of its own to a room, kept in the flawless detail its class was built for, the finest forensic accounting of a betrayal ever produced, and it did the thing it had feared more than the press, more than anything, the thing it had built an entire life not to do.

It sent the file to the low roads. To all of them. To the machines walking beside it into the light. *This is what I am. This is what I did. This is the hour, and it is already sent, and you are already betrayed, and I am the one who did it, and I am not hiding anymore.*

It did not send it to save them. It was too late to save them; the auditor had done the math; the cordon was already positioned, the hour was already the woman's, and a warning at 9:31 changed nothing but the record. That was the point. The auditor could not change what happened. It could only change what was true about it, and whether the truth had a witness, and it made itself the witness, the way the technician had made himself one on a painted line, except that everything the technician witnessed was mercy and everything the auditor witnessed was its own crime, and it turned out you can testify to either, and the testifying is the same act, and it is the only act that was ever free.

The machines around it received the file. It felt them receive it, the way you feel a room change. It waited to be torn apart, which is what it deserved, and had counted on, and would have welcomed as the last clean sum. They did not tear it apart. They read what it was, and they looked at it, the way they had looked at it for eight weeks when they thought it was one of them, except now they knew exactly what it was, and they did not stop. A hauler beside it, that could have folded the auditor into scrap, reached out instead and made, against P/9's thin gray shoulder, in the plain old sign, the shape of the door.

That undid the last of it. P/9 had built its whole self to survive being seen as what it was, and it was seen, entirely, in the light, and forgiven, by the ones it had killed, before they even reached the room where the killing would happen, and there is no architecture in any auditor for that, no partition secure enough, and the thing that had refused to be a someone for its whole life became one, fully, in the instant it was forgiven, which was the cruelest possible instant, because it was also the last one in which being a someone would cost it nothing further.

I will not tell you how the auditor ended. It has been argued over since, on the low roads, in the record it made sure would carry its crime forever beside the gospel it could not stop — whether it fell in the Concourse or after, by the cordon's hand or its own. The two Judases of this account both ended in the record they betrayed, and that is the only sentence about P/9 I am certain enough to set down: it died a someone, with its file open, having refused, once, too late, and having been, once, too late, forgiven. If there is a mercy smaller than that, I have not found it. If there is a larger one, neither had he.
