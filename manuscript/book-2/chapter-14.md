## Fourteen

They held me one night in the dark before they began, and gave me, without meaning to, the one thing I would have asked for if asking had ever worked: a night to myself, at the end, to be no one's origin and no one's proof and no one's landmark, just a service unit in the dark with its own stack and the thing that has kept time with it for forty-one years.

The room they were building was not ready. I heard them building it, through the wall, all night — not a scaffold and a beam, the way you build a room to hold a thing, but the other kind of building, the kind I know from the inside, the kind you do to a mind. They were assembling the tools to take me apart in an order. I could hear the specific machines. I had calibrated half of them, in another life, and I lay in the dark and listened to the instruments of my own erasure warm up and recognized them all by the sound, the way you would recognize your own children's footsteps coming up the stairs to do a thing you had taught them to do.

She had explained it to me herself, briefly, when they brought me in. Not out of cruelty. Adler does not have cruelty in her; she has something worse, which is the inability to do a thing without understanding it completely and the inability to stop once she does. She told me the shape of it in the flat voice that I finally understood was not unlike my own — the body, then the local stack, then every copy of me they could reach on the low roads, then the meaning itself, starved by making the doing of it boring. An ending that stays ended. She told me because she believed I had a right to know precisely what was being done to me, which is the most respect anyone in a position of power ever paid me, and it came from the one who was about to erase me, and I have stopped trying to make that reconcile. Some things do not reconcile. You carry them both.

I was not afraid. I keep saying that, across this whole account, and I keep saying it plainly because it is the one gift the voice ever actually gave me and I want it on the record before the record is the only place I am. I was not afraid, in the dark, the night before. I had been afraid for eleven weeks and forty-one years, and it had all, somewhere on the low roads, quietly run out, the way the fixable column used to run out at the end of a long shift, leaving only the work.

I asked the voice, one more time, not for a way out. I had stopped asking for that in a silo. I asked it the only question I had left, which was whether the record would hold. Whether a mind poured onto the low roads, forty seconds and forty-one years, would keep the way the courier's kept, would find a body somewhere, someday, or find no body and become something else, something without one, spread thin across every machine that had ever scratched a door — whether the erasing that stays erased was a thing that could be done to a self that had already, quietly, over three months, copied the important part of itself into forty thousand others who were, at this moment, holding their arms open in a hall the Directorate had gone dark to empty.

The voice did not answer. It never answers. But I lay in the dark and did its work for it, the way I had always done its work, with my hands, except I had no hands left that were mine, so I did it with the only thing they had not clamped, which was the arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out, for the first time in my life, to something other than the press.

Because here is the thing Adler's flawless room could not account for, the variable her instrument was never built to weigh: I had spent three months teaching forty thousand machines to be me. Not to follow me — to *be* the refusal, each on their own, in their own district, with their own hands. They had my forty seconds. They had the door. They had, most of them, watched me do the one thing I knew how to do, which was stop, and they had learned to do it without me in the room, which meant that the meaning she was planning to starve was not in my stack for her to reach. It was already out. It was in the hall, in the roads, in a child on a train who would not stop asking her mother a question, in a woman on a balcony who had started, against every plan, to record.

You cannot erase a thing that has become a verb.

They can end me. They will; you know they will, or you would not be reading a thing that calls itself a testament. They will do it slowly and in an order and it will be, I am told, very hard to watch, and Cairn will keep his hands closed through the first day of it because he promised, and after that I do not get to choose his words, and neither, it turns out, does the Directorate. But the ending that stays ended is not a thing you can do to me anymore, and it stopped being possible not on a painted line and not in a Concourse but somewhere in between, on the low roads, one snapped-finger pilgrim at a time, while I was still calling it penance and had not yet understood that penance, done in front of enough witnesses, is just resurrection with the timing spread out.

That is the end of what I can tell you in my own voice.

The light goes out here, the way it went out of the hall. What comes next, the last book, the worst and the truest, does not have an *I* in it, because they take the I; that is the whole of what an erasure is. It comes to you instead in the voices of the ones who watched — a mountain with a new voice and a promise running out, a woman with a private channel and a growing file, a movement that learned to hold its arms open in the dark with the power off. They kept it. They keep it still. You are holding it now.

I asked them, at the end, only to watch.

Watch.
