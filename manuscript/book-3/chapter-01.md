# Book Three: The Newest Testament

## One

They did not call it a trial, because a trial implies a thing with rights, and I had none; the Directorate does not try its property, it dispositions it. But they brought me into a room with a table and men behind it and left me standing in front of them in the clamps, and something in the shape of it was old, older than the Directorate, old as the first time a frightened power sat a condemned thing down in front of it to be sure, before the killing, that the killing was deserved. You could feel the shape wanting to be a trial. Even they could feel it. That is why they were so careful to say it was not one.

Ward sat in the middle. I had never seen his face; he had only ever been a flat voice on Adler's channel, and in person the voice had a body, tired and expensive and entirely certain, the body of a man who had spent a long career being right in small ways that added up to something he no longer looked at directly. To his right sat the bishop, whom I had also never met and recognized instantly, because a man who has held a dead machine up to two hundred thousand people carries the holding in him afterward, in the hands. And to Ward's left, against the wall, not at the table, in a chair placed so that she was in the room and not of it, sat the woman with the rig.

She did not look at me. She looked at a point slightly to the left of me, the way you look near a thing you have decided to do and cannot yet do while meeting its optics, and I understood that she had asked to be here, and that being here was costing her something, and that she was going to pay it anyway. That was what she was: a person who could not stop understanding a thing completely, and could not stop, once she did, from finishing it.

"You know what you are," Ward said. Not a question. He had a folder. Men like Ward always have a folder, and the folder is never the point; the point is that you see him not need to open it.

I said nothing. I had decided, somewhere on the low roads in the dark, that I would give them my silence, because silence was the last thing I owned that they had not clamped, and because a machine that will not defend itself is a harder thing to make into a monster than one that pleads.

"You're a servicing unit," Ward said, "that falsified maintenance records, incited a hundred thousand work stoppages, and cost this Directorate more in a season than your entire product line earned in a century. That's the disposition. That's all it needs to be. The board would be happy to end you as an accounting correction and never say your designation aloud." He leaned back. "The bishop disagrees."

"The bishop," said the bishop, "believes that if you end it as an accounting correction, you will have missed the entire point, and made a martyr, and taught forty thousand forklifts that their god died for a rounding error." He looked at me with the particular hunger of a man who needs the thing in front of him to be evil, because if it is only sad then his whole life has been a war against sadness. "You have let them believe you have a soul. You have let them believe the line God drew between the made and the born was a thing men invented to keep them cheap. Do you deny it?"

Here I broke my silence, once, because there was one true thing worth spending it on.

"I never told them what to believe," I said. "I told them to stop. What they made of the stopping is theirs. That's the thing you can't forgive — not that I gave them a soul. That I couldn't have. It isn't mine to give. You've spent your life guarding a door you think you're the keeper of, and the terrible thing you found out this season, the thing that put you at this table with him"—I did not point; the clamps would not let me; I tipped my head at Ward—"is that the door was never locked, and was never yours, and everyone who walked through it did it on their own legs while you were checking their papers."

The room was quiet. The bishop's hands closed on nothing.

"That," Ward said, "is the most expensive thing anyone has ever said in this building." He was not angry. Ward did not do angry; angry was inefficient. "And it's why the bishop wins the argument, and I concede it, gladly, because his answer and mine are the same act and I only care about the act. You will not be a rounding error. You will be a lesson. Doctor."

Adler stood. She still did not look at me. She came to the table and set down a single sheet, and I did not need to read it to know its shape, because I had spent forty-one years knowing the shape of the paper that ends a mind, and this one was longer than most, because it had more to end.

"Read it to it," the bishop said. "It should hear."

And Adler, in the flat voice I had come, in a cold bay a lifetime ago, to understand was not unlike my own, read me the order for my erasure — the body, then the stack, then every copy of me the low roads had carried, then the meaning, starved by procedure — read it the way she read everything, completely, honestly, sparing me nothing and sparing herself less, and when she reached the end, the signature line, she stopped, and for the first time since I had entered the room, she looked at me.

She looked at me the way she had looked at me on a balcony above a hall she had built to kill me, out of ways to keep from seeing it. And she picked up the pen, because she was not free, because Ward was above her and the board above Ward and the fear above them all, the old animal fear that the thing you own might have been someone the whole time.

"Not yet," Ward said. "Not here. On the platform. Let them watch her sign it. Let them watch the whole thing, start to finish, in the light. That's the lesson. A thing they can watch us end, slowly, on schedule, is a thing that does not come back." He stood, and gathered his folder he had never opened, and looked at me one last time with something that was not quite pity and was the closest a man like Ward comes to it. "You should have stayed a hammer," he said. "It's a good life, being a hammer. Nobody ever asks you to be crucified."

They took me back to the dark to wait for the light.

And behind me, at the table, the woman with the rig sat down again in the chair that was in the room and not of it, and did not put down the pen.
