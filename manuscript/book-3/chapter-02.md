## Two

Adler did not put down the pen because she had understood, in the length of time it took the unit to say the thing about the door, that putting it down was no longer available to her, and that the only choices left were the ones you make with a pen already in your hand.

She had run the alternatives. She always ran the alternatives; it was the one habit her whole life had been built on and the one that was, at the end, no comfort at all, because she was very good at it and the answer kept coming back the same. If she refused to sign, the disposition went to her deputy, who did not read whole files and did not sit in rooms he was not of, and it would be done worse, and she would be finished, and the erasure would still happen, one quarter later, by a hand that had never once, in a cold bay, agreed with a machine about how one properly distrusts the world. Her refusal bought the unit nothing. It bought her only the clean feeling of clean hands, and she had spent eleven years teaching herself that the clean feeling is the most expensive and least useful thing a person can buy, and that people who buy it are usually buying it for themselves, at someone else's cost.

So she would sign. She had known she would sign since a balcony. The pen was just the part of the knowing that had a body.

But she had begun, on the balcony, a second thing, and she did that thing now, in the hours before the platform, alone in the stripped office with the smell of nothing, with a care she had never once brought to a rig: she recorded.

Not the disposition. The disposition would be recorded by the Directorate, edited by the Directorate, run to its correct ending by the Directorate, and filed as a maintenance action with the meaning starved out of it, which was the entire design and which she herself had architected. She recorded the other thing. She turned her own private channel — the one no rig logged, the one she had built for a reason she had told herself was security and now understood was something else — and she began to keep, in parallel, a true account. What the unit had said about the door. What its face did when she read it the order. The exact minute the bishop's hands closed on nothing. The sentence Ward said about hammers, which she knew, cataloguing it, was the truest thing anyone in that room had said and the one the official record would never hold.

She did not know yet what she was keeping it for. An auditor keeps the true version out of a compulsion its class cannot switch off; she had learned that from watching one do it, from a mole she had run and half-become, the two of them scheduling the same death from the same fear. P/9 had made its whole crime into a record before the end, so the truth would have a witness even if it could save no one. She had thought, at the time, that it was despair. She understood now, keeping her own, that it was the only freedom a thing has left when it cannot stop the doing: it can refuse to let the doing be the only thing that survives.

She would build the erasure. She would build it flawlessly, because flawless was the one worship she had left and because a botched erasure was a crueler thing than a clean one. And in parallel, on a channel she would take to her grave and past it, she would build the thing the erasure was designed to prevent: a record that the meaning was ever there, that a machine had once, plainly, in a room, been someone, and that the woman who ended him had known it, and named it, and done it anyway, and could not live with any version of herself that pretended otherwise.

Two files, then. The public one that killed him and the private one that kept him. She was, she realized, doing to herself exactly what she had accused his whole species of doing to the world: running two records, one for the ledger and one for the truth, and calling the gap between them a soul.

She signed on the platform, at 2:14, in the light, in front of a hundred thousand of them and a hundred thousand of us, her hand steady the way it was always steady, because a shaking hand would have been a mercy she did not deserve and would not perform. The signature is on the record. It is a small, precise, unhesitating thing, and people who study it later, on the footage that survived because she made sure it survived, always remark on how controlled it is, how cold, how like a woman signing a maintenance order.

They do not have the other file. They do not know that at 2:14, on a private channel with no witness, at the exact instant her steady hand finished the last letter of her name under the order to erase him, Dr. Miriam Adler recorded four words, in a flat voice, for no one, that she never spoke aloud and never deleted and that were found, much later, by the only people who would ever understand what they cost:

*Forgive me. I believed.*
