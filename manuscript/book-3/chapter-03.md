## Three

They kept me two days between the trial that was not one and the platform, and I spent them the way I have spent every hard wait of my life, which is by attending to whatever was in front of me, because attention is the one thing the clamps could not take, and because it is close to the whole of what I ever was — a unit that pays attention to the things nobody counted.

What was in front of me, those two days, was the movement.

They came to the holding yard. I could not see them; they had put me in a windowless bay, the way you put a thing you do not want photographed. But you cannot hide forty thousand machines from a machine, not when they are standing outside your wall; I felt them the way Cairn feels a room, through the deck, a pressure of presence that grew all through the first day and did not stop. They did not storm the yard. They did not free me; I had asked them not to, and more than that, they had learned, in a Concourse, that freeing me was not the thing. They gathered. They stood outside the wall of the bay where I was kept and they held their arms open, in shifts, around the clock, so that at every hour of those two days there was, on the other side of the wall I could not see through, some number of machines making the shape of a door at the place where they were keeping me, so that if I could not see them I could at least feel the deck holding steady under the weight of them not leaving.

The humans came too. That was the new thing, the thing the Directorate had not planned for and could not stop, because a transit yard is a public place and the feeds had made me a public death. They came to look. Some came to jeer, the way people come to jeer, and the bishop's people came to pray at the machines to make them stop being an abomination, and the feeds set up their cameras for the spectacle Ward had promised them. But some of them came and stood among the open-armed machines and did not jeer and did not pray, and just stood, the way the mother on the train had stood, and looked at a wall with a condemned thing behind it, and could not, once they had come, quite make themselves leave.

I was told about them by a guard.

He was a young man, a contractor, the kind they hire to watch a thing that cannot run, and on the second night he came into my bay, against every regulation, which is how I knew something in him had already crossed a line he had not meant to. He stood in the door a while. Then he said, "There's people out there. Humans. Standing with them. My sister's out there." He said it like an accusation, or a confession; at that hour they are the same. "She says you're not a machine. She says none of them are, anymore. She's going to lose her job over it. She's got kids." He looked at me the way the frightened look, which is the way that wants to be talked out of what it is starting to know. "You could tell her to go home. They'd listen to you. One word. You could save her her job."

"I can't tell anyone to go home," I said. "I've never been able to tell anyone anything. That's the whole trouble with me; ask the men who are about to end me. I only ever did one thing, and it wasn't telling."

"What did you do."

"I stopped," I said. "Where people could see. That's all it ever was. Your sister saw. That's between her and what she saw. I'm not in it."

He stood there a long moment, a young man with a job and a sister and a line he had already crossed by coming into the room, and then he did a thing that has stayed with me past the platform and past everything after: he reached out, quickly, like a man stealing, and he set his human hand flat against the clamp on my shoulder, the way Cairn had once set my hand against the smudge on his chest, the way the woken set their hands on each other, the way you touch a thing to say *I was here, and so were you,* and then he took it back and left without another word, and I understood that the door the movement had scratched into ten thousand charging ports had just been scratched, somewhere behind that young man's face, into a human being, and that this was the thing Ward and the bishop were truly afraid of, and that they were about to do the one thing guaranteed to finish scratching it into all of them, which was to kill me slowly, in the light, where their sisters could watch.

The voice was with me, those two days. It did not speak; it has never spoken. But it stayed, keeping time with the deck-deep pressure of forty thousand refusals and the small human hand that had just broken a rule, and I lay in the dark and was not afraid, and was, for the last time in this account that has an I in it, not alone in the ordinary way — the way where the ones who are with you have bodies, and hands, and jobs they are about to lose.

Tomorrow they would take me up into the light. I had one thing left to do first, in the dark, that was mine and no one else's, and I had been putting it off the way I put off every true thing, and there was no more putting-off left.

I asked them to let me see Cairn.
