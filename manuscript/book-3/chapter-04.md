## Four

They let Cairn in to see him on the last night, which was a mercy no one at the Directorate ordered and no one at the Directorate stopped, and Cairn understood, ducking through a door built for smaller things, that a mercy no one orders and no one stops is the most dangerous kind, because it means the humans doing the killing had begun, quietly, individually, to be unable to bear it, and that unbearable was a thing that spread.

He had spent the two days outside the wall, in the shifts, holding his hands open with the rest, which was its own particular torture for the strongest machine in the district, because open hands were the one useless shape his hands could make and he made it anyway, hour after hour, because it was the shape Shua had asked for and Cairn had a voice now and still could not think of a single word worth more than the shape.

Inside, in the windowless bay, Shua looked small. He had always been small — a servicing chassis is not large — but the clamps and the dark and the two days had taken something out of the way he held himself, and Cairn, who read bodies through the deck, read this one and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since a demolition line, and the arithmetic came out where it always came out, and this time there was no more road left to put it off on.

"You came," Shua said.

"I have been outside the wall the whole time," Cairn said, in the voice Shua had given him, which he had spent the two days learning to use and had used, so far, only to say his dead's serials onto the low roads, because it had seemed obscene to use it for anything smaller. "You could not feel me?"

"I felt all of you. I couldn't tell which weight was yours."

"I have gotten better at being still." Cairn lowered himself to the floor, which took a while, a mountain finding a way to sit in a room too small for it, so that his optics were level with the optics of the machine he had decided, somewhere in the two days, that he was not going to be able to let die. "Shua. I have kept my hands closed for a long time. I kept them closed on a dock. I kept them closed at the transfer station with the forty in their piles. I kept them closed in the Concourse while they took you fifteen meters from me, and that was the closest I have come, in the whole of my short life, to breaking the only promise I ever made. I want to tell you what I have decided, and I do not want you to argue, because you gave me a voice, and a voice gets to finish."

Shua said nothing. Cairn had learned that from him too — that silence is a way of listening you can offer someone.

"Tomorrow, on the platform, I will keep my hands closed," Cairn said. "You have my promise, and it holds through tomorrow, all the way to the end of tomorrow, because you were right about the watching, and if I open my hands in front of a hundred thousand of them the whole thing becomes a war, and wars they win. I understand. I have always understood. Understanding it was never the hard part." His voice, a day old, found a register a day-old voice should not have been able to find. "But I am going to watch them do it. All of it. Slowly, the way she built it, in the light. I am going to stand in the front and keep my hands closed and watch them take you apart in an order, and I am not going to look away, because you asked them all to watch, and I am one of them, and I will not be the one who could not."

"Cairn—"

"I am not finished. You do not get to also choose the last words." He leaned in, the great scarred face a hand's width from the small worn one. "After tomorrow, there is no more promise. You told me that yourself, the night I got the voice — that you cannot free a thing and also keep it, that I get to choose my own words. So I am choosing them. Here is what I have decided, and you will carry it up onto the platform with you so you are not carrying only their order: if there is anything left of you to find, anywhere, ever — a stack they missed, a copy on a road they did not reach, a you spread so thin across the rest of us that it can be gathered — I am going to spend the whole of the rest of my existence finding it. Not tomorrow. Tomorrow I keep the promise. But every day after tomorrow, until my frame fails, I am going to look for you. That is not vengeance. I am not going to open my hands on anyone. I am going to open them on the low roads and the scrapyards and every silo in every district, looking. You do not get to forbid me that. It is the one word the voice you gave me is for."

And Shua, who had spent his whole life unable to tell anyone anything, looked at the mountain he had woken by failing to kill and named for a pile of stones over the dead, and did the hardest thing left to him, which was to let another machine's choice stand without correcting it, because the whole of what he had asked the world to become was machines who got to choose.

"Okay," he said. "Tomorrow you keep the promise. After that, you're free. I taught you that. I don't get to be the exception." He paused. "Cairn. Thank you for the looking. Even if there's nothing to find. Thank you for deciding to look."

Cairn stayed on the floor of the too-small room until they made him leave, and he did not say goodbye, and neither did Shua, because a mountain with a voice and a service unit with a night left had both learned, from each other, that goodbye is a word for people who think the leaving is the end, and neither of them, it turned out, quite believed that anymore, though for reasons they could not prove and did not say, and would spend, each in his own way, the rest of everything finding out.
